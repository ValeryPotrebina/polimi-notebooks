62 cycles, one at a time: the scoreboard example and the exam table

After this lesson you will recompute the notes' 62-cycle example from the four rules of the scoreboard, point at the table cell that holds each waiting instruction, and fill the January 2026 scoreboard table in both of its schemes — with the CPI and the speedup.

In the notes' example the last instruction, FADD F6, F8, F2, finishes computing at cycle 16. Its sum is correct and sits inside the Add unit. The scoreboard lets it write F6 only at cycle 22: five full cycles with the answer in hand. Nothing is wrong with the adder and nothing is wrong with the sum. Why the ban, what would break if the write went through at 17, and which single cell of the three tables enforces it?

A second puzzle, from the paper of 8 January 2026 (reproduced in the notes from page 479): seven instructions, row I0 given as 1 / 2 / 5 / 6. On the basic scoreboard they take 25 cycles. Switch on forwarding and postpone the WAW check to write-back and they take 21. Which of the two switches is worth more — and why do their savings not add up?

Both answers are one table cell each. First the run, cycle by cycle.

Six instructions, five units, four rules

The same example, with the same latencies 1, 2, 10, 40, is in Hennessy and Patterson's Appendix C and on the Berkeley CS252 slides: you will meet it again.

The example is the one the notes walk on pages 115–139, one page per cycle, with the cells that changed highlighted in yellow. Here it is in the mnemonics of the exam papers; the notes write it in Hennessy and Patterson's spelling (LD, MULTD, SUBD, DIVD, ADDD), which means the same thing.

I0: LD   F6, 34(R2)
I1: LD   F2, 45(R3)
I2: FMUL F0, F2, F4   ; RAW F2
I3: FSUB F8, F6, F2   ; RAW F6, F2
I4: FDIV F10, F0, F6  ; RAW F0, F6
I5: FADD F6, F8, F2   ; RAW F8, F2
                      ; WAW F6, WAR F6

The machine: one Integer unit for the loads (latency 1 — every access is a cache hit), one Add unit for FADD and FSUB (2 cycles), two multipliers Mult1 and Mult2 (10 cycles), one Divide unit (40 cycles). The scheme is the basic one of lesson 9: WAW checked at Issue, no forwarding. The notes' Figure 25 (page 109) carries other latencies, 1, 4, 7, 7 and 24; they are illustrative. Latencies always come from the exercise text.

Before touching a table, list the dependences, as the exam's dependency-analysis question does. Seven true dependences: F6 feeds I3 and I4; F2 feeds I2, I3 and I5; F0 feeds I4; F8 feeds I5. One output dependence: I0 and I5 both write F6. Two anti-dependences: I3 and I4 read F6 before I5 overwrites it. Figure 1 draws them, and marks the chain that will decide the whole count.

Every number of the run comes from the four rules of lesson 9, which the stepper there ran on a smaller fragment. Restated in one line each, in the form you will use them:

  • Issue — the unit is not Busy and Result[D] is empty: a unit of the right class is free and nobody earlier still has to write my destination (WAW). A unit is Busy from Issue through Write Result, so it is free again the cycle after its WB.
  • Read Operands — \(RR \ge WB_P + 1\) for every source, \(P\) its producer: the value must be in the register file, there is no forwarding. Both sources are read at once, and never before \(I + 1\).
  • Execute — \(E = RR + L\), with \(L\) the latency of the unit. The notes' Time column counts the execution cycles still to go: set to \(L\) at the read, it reaches 0 in cycle \(E\).
  • Write Result — \(WB \ge E + 1\), and \(WB \ge RR_A + 1\) for every earlier instruction \(A\) that still has to read my destination (WAR). The test reads the unit rows: a row with my destination as a source and its flag Rj or Rk still Yes says no.

"LD F6, 34(R2) is issued and begins execution." Not in cycle 1: it only issues. It reads R2 in cycle 2 and accesses memory in cycle 3 — the table on the same page is right (Issue = 1, nothing else). Every row of the exam table starts this way: one cycle to issue, one to read, then the latency.

Four questions fill a row: is my unit free and my destination unpromised; when was the last of my sources written; add the latency; has every earlier reader of my destination read it.

Cycles 1 to 12: a busy unit and the RAW jungle

The paper's column names are I, RR, E, WB. The notes' chapter writes Issue, Read Op., Exec Comp, Write Res. Same four stages.

Cycle 1. I0 issues on the Integer unit; the register result entry of F6 becomes "Integer". Cycle 2. I0 reads R2. I1 wants to issue. It depends on nobody — but the only Integer unit is Busy: a structural hazard, and because issue is in order nothing behind I1 moves either. Cycle 3. I0 accesses memory, its one execution cycle. Cycle 4. I0 writes F6. The unit is still Busy in its write cycle, so I1 waits once more. Three cycles lost on the one unit that both loads need; keep the number, it comes back at the end.

Pages 120 to 122 print F3 as the base register of the second load. Read R3: an integer register holds the address, as R2 does in the first load's row on page 116. The notes put a load's base register under Fk, Hennessy and Patterson under Fj; either is fine if you say which.

Cycle 5. I1 issues; F2 is promised to the Integer unit. Cycle 6. I1 reads R3. I2 (FMUL) issues on Mult1 — the unit is free and nobody is due to write F0 — and copies "Integer" into its Qj: Rj = No, it waits for F2. Cycle 7. I1 completes (a hit). I3 (FSUB) issues on the Add unit: F6 is in the register file (Rj = Yes), F2 is not (Qk = Integer, Rk = No). Cycle 8. I1 writes F2, and the write wakes two rows at once: Rj of Mult1 and Rk of Add flip to Yes. I4 (FDIV) issues on the Divide unit and waits for F0 from Mult1. A cascade is forming: FDIV waits for FMUL, which waited for the load.

Cycle 9. FMUL and FSUB read their operands in the same cycle — the notes assume a register file with enough read ports ("e.g., 4 read ports", page 124) — and the Time counters start at 10 and 2. I5 (FADD) cannot issue: not because of F6, whose register result entry has been blank since cycle 4, but because the Add unit is Busy with FSUB. The second structural stall. Cycle 11. FSUB completes: Time = 0, E = 11. Cycle 12. FSUB writes F8. Its WAR test passes trivially — no row lists F8 as an unread source; FADD is not even in the machine — and the Add unit is free from cycle 13.

Functional unit status, row Add: "1 | Add | Yes | Sub | F8 | F6 | F2 | | Integer | Yes | Yes"

One cycle after its read the row is Add | Yes | Sub | F8 | F6 | F2 | — | — | No | No. Read Operands sets Rj and Rk to No — they mean ready and not yet read — and Qk = Integer names a producer that wrote F2 two cycles earlier. Both slips run on: the flags stay Yes in every executing row up to page 138 (Mult1 in this very table), and Qk = Integer stays from page 123, where the same write of F2 already cleared Mult1's Qj, to page 126.

Hennessy and Patterson reset the flags at Read Operands (Appendix C, Figure C.57; the convention of lesson 9 and its red pen on page 113), and the CS252 slides of this very example print "No No" and a blank Qk from cycle 10 on. The WAR test reads these flags: FADD may write F6 at 22 only because Rk in the Divide row flipped to No at FDIV's read in cycle 21. With the flags left at Yes, the test would hold FADD until FDIV's own write at 62 clears the Divide row — and the notes' own table, ADDD written at 22, would be impossible. State the H&P convention on your sheet. (One more stale cell, page 119: "Integer" under F6 in cycle 4, the cycle in which F6 is written; the notes clear F2 at cycle 8 and F8 at cycle 12 in the write cycle.)

"The key idea here is that the scoreboard only allows one instruction to be written per cycle; even completed executions must wait their turn."

FSUB writes at 12 because Write Result is a stage of its own, one cycle after the cycle in which execution completes (\(WB \ge E + 1\)). Nothing else writes in cycle 11.

Look at the table: the only other instruction executing, FMUL, has Time = 8 in cycle 11. A single write port would matter only if two results were ready to be written in the same cycle — it never happens in this example (the six writes fall in cycles 4, 8, 12, 20, 22 and 62).

Sixty-two cycles on one slider

The notes' example on the basic scoreboard: the instruction status table as it stands at the end of the chosen cycle (cells that changed in that cycle are highlighted), the timeline below it, and the log of what happened. The switches change the machine: forwarding, the postponed WAW check, a second load unit, a second adder, a shorter divide. The total and the CPI follow.

  1. Before you move anything: in which cycle does I1, the second load, issue, and which cell of the tables holds it until then? Then press Next cycle up to cycle 5 and read the log of each cycle on the way.
    What you should see

    Cycle 5. The Integer row says Busy = Yes from cycle 1 through cycle 4 — the cycle in which I0 writes — so the structural check at Issue fails three times. No data is involved: I1 depends on nobody.

  2. Go to cycle 9. Predict which two instructions read their operands in this cycle, and why I5 cannot issue although the register result entry of F6 is blank.
    What you should see

    FMUL and FSUB both read: both were waiting for the same write of F2 at cycle 8. I5 is refused by the Add row's Busy = Yes — FSUB holds the unit through its write at 12 — not by a WAW, which ended when I0 wrote F6 at cycle 4.

  3. Step from 16 to 22 and watch the bar of I5. Predict what the dashed segment after its execution means and in which cycle it ends.
    What you should see

    Execution is over at 16; the dashed bar is the WAR wait at Write Result, cycles 17 to 21: FDIV has not read F6. It ends at 22, the cycle after FDIV reads (21). The hazard delays only the write.

  4. Now the switches, one at a time, with a prediction before each: forwarding only; a second load unit only; both; a second adder only.
    What you should see

    60, 59, 57 and 62. Forwarding takes one cycle out of each of the two RAW links of the chain (F2 → FMUL, F0 → FDIV); the second load unit removes the three-cycle structural stall of I1; the second adder changes nothing, because FSUB and FADD are not on the chain — FADD issues at 9 instead of 13 and still writes at 22, behind FDIV's read.

Three of the 62 cycles are lost at the very start: the second load, the head of the critical chain, waits for the only Integer unit, which is Busy through the first load's write result.

Cycles 13 to 22: an out-of-order read, and a write that must wait

Cycle 13. I5 (FADD) issues on the Add unit: no structural hazard any more, and the register result entry of F6 is blank — the WAW with I0 was over at cycle 4. Its sources: F8, written at 12, so Rj = Yes; F2, written at 8, so Rk = Yes. Nothing holds it. Cycle 14. FADD reads F8 and F2 — before FDIV, issued at 8, has read anything. This is the out-of-order read that the split of ID was built for: each issued instruction waits only for its own two flags. Cycles 15–16. FADD executes; E = 16.

Cycle 17. FADD asks to write F6. The Write Result test scans the unit rows for a row that has F6 as a source with its flag still Yes — and finds one: the Divide row, Fk = F6, Rk = Yes. FDIV, an earlier instruction, has not read the old F6; it is still waiting for F0. Refused. The sum stays inside the Add unit, the unit stays Busy, and nothing else is touched: issue is long over, and no other instruction wants the adder. The notes' page 132 says it exactly: "WAR hazards delay write-back, not execution."

What would break. FDIV computes F0 / F6 and, because it comes before FADD in program order, must see the F6 that I0 loaded. If FADD wrote at 17, FDIV at 21 would read F8 + F2 — a value from an instruction that comes after it. (In this particular program F8 + F2 = (F6 − F2) + F2 happens to be the loaded F6 again in exact arithmetic; floating-point rounding would expose the difference, and the hardware neither knows nor cares: it protects the order of the names, not the values.)

Cycles 17–19. Mult1's Time, 3 at the end of cycle 16, goes 2, 1, 0: FMUL completes at 19, not 18. Cycle 20. FMUL writes F0. Its own WAR test looks at the Divide row: Fj = F0, but Rj = No — FDIV is waiting for exactly this write — so the write is allowed; it flips Rj to Yes and clears the register result entry of F0. Cycle 21. FDIV reads F0 and F6; both flags go to No; Time = 40. Cycle 22. FADD's test now passes (Rk = No in the Divide row) and it writes F6. FDIV executes from 22 to 61 and writes F10 at 62. Figure 2 is the before and after of the one cell that decided.

The write that must wait

Cycles 16 to 22 of the run, one per step, as the two rows that matter stand at the end of each cycle, with the Write Result test for FADD spelled out underneath. The second switch breaks the rule and lets FADD write as soon as it is done, so you can see what FDIV would read.

  1. Before stepping: name the single cell that forbids FADD's write at the end of cycle 16 — table, row, column, value. Then look for the orange frame.
    What you should see

    Functional-unit status, row Divide, column Rk, value Yes: FDIV lists F6 as its second source and has not read it. The register result status (F6 = Add) is not consulted; it only knows who will write F6.

  2. Predict the cycle at the end of which the frame disappears, and what happens in that cycle. Then step until it does.
    What you should see

    End of cycle 21: FDIV reads F0 and F6, and both flags go to No. The flag could not flip earlier, because FDIV could not read before FMUL had written F0 at 20. FADD writes in the next cycle, 22.

  3. Switch on "break the rule" and step to cycle 21. What does FDIV read as F6, and what does that mean for the program?
    What you should see

    The value FADD wrote at 17 — the result of an instruction that comes after FDIV in program order. The sequential meaning of the program is broken; the hardware has no way to know that in this program the two values happen to coincide. That is what a WAR hazard is, and why the writer waits.

  4. With the rule broken or kept, does the E column of FADD change?
    What you should see

    No: E = 16 either way. The hazard moves one number only, the WB of the writer; execution is never delayed by a WAR. The notes' page 128 says the opposite for cycle 13 — see the red pen below.

"Still blocked by a WAR hazard on F6: DIVD is supposed to read F6, but ADDD wants to write it. ADDD must wait until DIVD reads F6 to avoid overwriting it too early."

At cycle 13 nothing blocks ADDD (our I5): it reads its operands at 14 and executes in 15–16. The WAR delays only its write, cycles 17 to 21.

The tables of the notes say so themselves: Read Op. = 14, Exec Comp = 16 on page 131, and page 132 states the rule correctly: "WAR hazards delay write-back, not execution". The widget above shows E = 16 with the rule kept and with the rule broken.

"MULTD writes to F0." — "This finally allows DIVD to read operands in the next cycle (20)."

In cycle 19 MULTD completes execution (its own table: Exec Comp = 19, Time = 0). It writes F0 at 20, DIVD reads at 21, and ADDD writes F6 at 22. The narration of pages 133–136 runs one cycle ahead of its tables: page 133 has MULTD "complete" at 18 with Time still 1, page 135 has DIVD "read" at 20, page 136 has F0 "written by MULTD in cycle 19".

Execution occupies the \(L\) cycles after the read: \(E = RR + L = 9 + 10 = 19\); Write Result is the next stage, \(WB = 20\); with no forwarding the consumer reads at \(WB + 1 = 21\); and the writer behind a WAR writes at \(RR + 1 = 22\). The final table on page 139 (6–9–19–20 and 8–21–61–62) is right; trust the tables, not the prose.

"ADDD writes result to F6: this completes the final WAW dependency involving F6."

What ends at cycle 22 is the WAR on F6: DIVD read the old F6 at 21, ADDD overwrites it at 22. The WAW on F6 (LD F6 → ADDD) never caused a stall — LD F6 wrote at cycle 4, nine cycles before ADDD issued.

A WAW hazard is checked at Issue against the register result table; when ADDD issued at 13 the entry of F6 was blank. A WAR hazard is checked at Write Result against the unit rows; that is the test that failed from 17 to 21. The Hazards Type column of the exam table asks you to tell the two apart.

A WAR hazard moves one number of the table, the writer's WB. The cell that decides is the earlier reader's flag — Rk = Yes in the Divide row — and it flips at that reader's RR.

Where the 62 cycles went: the chain, the CPI and four what-ifs

The whole run in the format of the exam, recomputed cell by cell with the rules above (and by the engine of the widget, which agrees with the notes' final table on page 139):

The CPI is the cycle of the last write result over the instruction count, as the formula sheet defines it: \(62 / 6 \approx 10.3\). Ten cycles per instruction on a machine that can overlap — because one chain of true dependences runs from the second load to the end, and nothing is left to overlap with its last link, the 40-cycle divide. Follow the chain:

  1. I1 issues at 5, not 2: the single Integer unit is Busy through I0's write at 4. It reads at 6, completes at 7, writes F2 at 8:

    \[ WB_1 = 8 \quad (\text{3 cycles structural}) . \]

  2. I2 may read F2 only after it is in the register file (no forwarding), then multiplies for 10 cycles and writes the cycle after:

    \[ \begin{aligned} RR_2 &= 8 + 1 = 9, \\ E_2 &= 9 + 10 = 19, \quad WB_2 = 20 . \end{aligned} \]

  3. I4 needs F0 and waits at Read Operands from 9 to 20; it reads at 21, divides for 40 cycles, writes the cycle after:

    \[ \begin{aligned} RR_4 &= 20 + 1 = 21, \\ E_4 &= 21 + 40 = 61, \quad WB_4 = 62 . \end{aligned} \]

  4. Add it up: the two loads back to back on the one Integer unit (4 + 4 = 8), then a read, the multiply, a write, a read, the divide and a write.

    \[ 62 = 8 + 1 + 10 + 1 + 1 + 40 + 1 . \]

    I3 and I5 finish inside the shadow of this chain; so does the five-cycle WAR wait of I5, which ends at 22, when the divide has only just started.

Because only the chain matters, each change to the machine can be judged by one question: which rule on the chain does it attack? The widget's switches give the numbers; here is why each is what it is.

Read the notes' closing paragraph (page 139) with this table in mind. It calls the example "a demonstration of: efficient hazard management, robust scheduling logic, clean and scalable hardware coordination"; the same run, read with Hennessy and Patterson, shows rather what limits the scoreboard, the list of lesson 9: a structural stall on the first instruction that could have overlapped, a five-cycle WAR wait that only renaming removes, one extra cycle on every RAW link because there is no forwarding, and a CPI above 10. The next algorithm, Tomasulo's (lesson 11), attacks all three: several reservation stations per unit, so the second load can issue while the first is still in flight; results renamed to those stations, so no WAR wait; and a common data bus that hands each result to its consumers as it is written.

62 = 8 + 1 + 10 + 1 + 1 + 40 + 1: the critical chain LD F2 → FMUL → FDIV; everything else finishes in its shadow, and a change that does not touch the chain changes nothing.

Exercise 1 of the paper of 8 January 2026 (reproduced in the notes from page 479) is the only scoreboard exercise among the papers of this course, and it is the model you just ran with the units and the latencies as parameters. Part A: "SCOREBOARD BASIC SCHEME … Check for WAW hazards in the ISSUE stage; No Forwarding; 2 LOAD/STORE Units (LDU1, LDU2) with latency 3 cycles; 1 FP Unit (FPU1) with latency 3 cycles; 1 ALU/BR Unit (ALU1) with latency 1 cycle." Then: "Calculate the CPI of the program and draw the timing diagram showing the status of each instruction at each clock cycle."

I0: LD    F2, VB(R6)  ; given: 1-2-5-6, LDU1
I1: FADD  F4, F2, F6
I2: SD    F4, VA(R7)
I3: LD    F4, VC(R6)
I4: SD    F4, VC(R7)
I5: ADDUI R6, R6, 4
I6: ADDUI R7, R7, 4

(The notes print the registers of this paper as $F2, $R6, the way the 2024 and 2025 papers do; the June 2026 paper and this notebook drop the dollar sign.) The given row is a gift: it fixes the conventions. E is the cycle in which execution completes, \(E = RR + 3\); the write comes the cycle after; a store has an E and a WB like everything else (its WB is the cycle its memory write completes) but no destination register, so no WAW is ever checked against it; and, as in the example, a unit is free again the cycle after its WB. The dependences, before anything else: true dependences on F2 (I0 → I1) and on F4 (I1 → I2, I3 → I4); an output dependence on F4 (I1, I3); anti-dependences on F4 (I2 reads it, I3 overwrites it), on R6 (I5 after I0, I3) and on R7 (I6 after I2, I4); and two load/store units for four memory instructions, one FP unit, one ALU.

Where the cycles go. I1 waits four cycles for F2 and I2 eight for F4: a load, an add and a store in a straight RAW chain, one register-file round trip each. I3 is the expensive row: at cycle 4 both load/store units are Busy; when LDU1 frees at 7 the register result entry of F4 still says FPU1, so the WAW with I1 holds it at Issue until I1's write at 11 — and, issue being in order, holds I4, I5 and I6 behind it although none of them waits for I1. Its WB at 17 passes the WAR test because I2 read F4 at 12. I4 then finds both units Busy again and takes LDU2, the first to free (I2's WB at 16). I6 waits for the single ALU. Last WB 25: \(\mathrm{CPI} = 25/7 \approx 3.57\).

Part B keeps everything and adds two "Optimizations: Check for WAW postponed to the WRITE BACK phase; Forwarding", asking for the table, the stalls, the CPI "and the speedup with respect to the previous case". Each one changes exactly one rule. Forwarding: a consumer reads in the cycle in which the producer writes, \(RR \ge WB_P\) instead of \(WB_P + 1\) — the result leaves the unit at the end of the producer's last execution cycle and is bypassed around the register file. That is how the notes' solution reads the paper's one word "Forwarding" (I0 writes at 6, I1 reads at 6); that solution is the note author's, not an official one, so write the convention you use next to your table. WAW postponed: Issue no longer looks at the register result table, so a second writer issues as soon as a unit is free; Write Result gains a second test, \(WB \ge WB_W + 1\) for every earlier writer \(W\) of the same register, next to the WAR test it already had — the explicit ordering of the two writes that lesson 9 said the hardware needs once the WAW check leaves Issue.

Now the second puzzle. Switch the two on one at a time and recompute (the trainer below checks you): forwarding alone gives 23 cycles, the postponed WAW check alone 22, both 21. The postponed check is worth more because it attacks the stall that blocks the queue: I3 issues at 7 instead of 12, and I4, I5, I6 move with it. Forwarding trims one cycle from each RAW link on the way to the end, and there are only two: I0 → I1 and I1 → I2, whose write frees LDU2 for I4. And the savings do not add up — 2 + 3 would be 5, both together save 4 — because both shorten the same wait, the issue of I4. In the basic scheme I4 waits for LDU2, freed at the end of the chain I0 → I1 → I2, and forwarding shortens that chain by two cycles. Once I3 issues at 7, I4 waits for LDU1 instead, freed by I3's write, and forwarding has only one cycle left to take: in the postponed-only variant I3 completes at 11 but I2 reads F4 only at 12, a genuine WAR stall that puts I3's write at 13; with forwarding I2 reads at 10 and I3 writes at 12.

At cycle 8 both load/store units are Busy: LDU1 with I3 through its WB at 12, LDU2 with I2 through its WB at 14. Two rules settle every case. A unit is taken when it is free, not by recency: run the trainer on Part B, where I3 holds LDU1 from 7 to 12 and I2 holds LDU2 from 3 to 14. And a unit is freed by the write-back, never by the end of execution: Busy lasts through WB, and the next issue comes the cycle after. Page 488 repeats the second slip for ALU1, freed by I5 "which completed its Execute stage in cycle 17" — I5's E is 16, its WB 17, and I6 issues at 18.

Explain the main concepts and benefits of each one of the following techniques of a scoreboard-based CPU, saying for each which hazard it addresses and what it cannot fix: 1. splitting the decode stage into Issue and Read Operands; 2. checking WAW hazards at write-back instead of at issue; 3. forwarding; 4. in-order issue with out-of-order completion.

Show model answer

1. Issue decodes and checks the resources (a free unit, no pending write to the destination: structural and WAW hazards), in order; Read Operands waits for the sources to be in the register file (RAW), separately for every issued instruction. Benefit: an instruction waits in the row of its own unit while independent later instructions issue behind it and may read before it (in the notes' example FADD, issued at 13, reads at 14 while FDIV, issued at 8, reads at 21). Cannot fix: a stall at Issue still blocks every later instruction.

2. The register result table is not consulted at Issue, so a later writer of a busy register issues as soon as a unit is free; Write Result then enforces the order of the two writes (WB of the second after WB of the first) in addition to the WAR test. Benefit: the in-order issue queue is not blocked by an output dependence (January 2026: I3 issues at 7 instead of 12 and the three instructions behind it issue 3 cycles earlier: 25 → 22). Cannot fix: the dependence itself — the two writes are still serialised, and the WAR test can still delay the second write; only renaming removes it.

3. A result is bypassed from the unit to a waiting consumer in the cycle it is written instead of after the register-file write: each RAW wait is one cycle shorter (RR = WB of the producer). Benefit: one cycle per link of every true-dependence chain (January 2026: 25 → 23). Cannot fix: the latency itself, and nothing about structural, WAW or WAR waits.

4. Instructions enter in program order, which keeps the hardware simple (the instruction being issued is always the later writer, so one lookup settles a WAW), and leave as soon as they are done, which lets independent instructions overlap long latencies (in the notes' example the subtract and the add complete inside the load–multiply–divide chain, which alone sets the 62 cycles). Cannot fix: WAR and WAW stalls (no renaming), imprecise exceptions (no reorder buffer), and the stalls of the issue queue behind a blocked instruction.

Four short lines per technique are enough: what it is, which hazard it addresses, what it buys (with a number from an exercise if you have one) and what it does not fix. The last line is what separates a full answer from a definition.

Key take-aways

  • The 62-cycle run follows from four rules: issue when a unit is free and the destination unpromised; read at the producer's WB + 1; complete at RR + L; write at E + 1 and after every earlier reader's RR — with a unit Busy through its WB.
  • Three cycles are lost at the very start: the second load, which heads the critical chain, waits for the only Integer unit. Structural stalls at Issue block the whole queue.
  • A WAR hazard moves one number, the writer's WB: FADD completes at 16 and writes at 22 because the Divide row shows Rk = Yes for F6 until FDIV reads at 21. Execution is never delayed by a WAR.
  • The critical chain LD F2 → FMUL → FDIV accounts for all 62 cycles (8 + 1 + 10 + 1 + 1 + 40 + 1); a change that does not touch it — a second adder — changes nothing, while forwarding (60), a second load unit (59) or both (57) do.
  • The notes' instruction-status tables are right and their narration of cycles 13 and 18–22 is not: FADD is not blocked at 13; FMUL completes at 19, writes at 20; FDIV reads at 21; FADD writes at 22, ending a WAR, not a WAW. In the unit-status tables, flags go back to No at the read.
  • January 2026: the basic scheme takes 25 cycles (CPI 25/7 ≈ 3.57); forwarding alone 23, the WAW check at write-back alone 22, both 21 (CPI 3.0, speedup 25/21 ≈ 1.19). The postponed check wins because it unblocks the in-order issue queue.
  • Under the optimisations exactly two rules change: RR ≥ WB of the producer, and the WAW test moves from Issue to Write Result (WB after the earlier writer's WB). The WAR test and the Busy rule stay.
Basic scheme (WAW at Issue, no forwarding)
\(I_n\) = max of \(I_{n-1}+1\), \(WB_U+1\), \(WB_W+1\); \(RR_n = \max(I_n+1,\ WB_P+1)\); \(E_n = RR_n + L\); \(WB_n = \max(E_n+1,\ RR_A+1)\) — \(U\) previous instruction on the unit, \(W\) pending earlier writer of the destination, \(P\) producer of each source, \(A\) earlier readers of the destination
Forwarding
\(RR_n = \max(I_n+1,\ WB_P)\): the consumer reads in the producer's write cycle
WAW check at write-back
drop \(WB_W+1\) from \(I_n\); add \(WB_n \ge WB_W+1\) at Write Result (the WAR test stays)
Unit Busy
from I through WB inclusive; a new issue on it at WB + 1; the first free unit of the class is taken
Rj, Rk
ready and not yet read: Yes when the producer writes, No at the read — the WAR test looks for a Yes
