Graphs, critical paths and the exam table: list scheduling

After this lesson you will draw the dependence graph of a loop body with its latencies, find its critical path, rank its instructions by priority and fill the paper's Memory / Floating Point / Integer table cycle by cycle — with the four numbers the exercise asks for.

The paper of 8 January 2026, solved on pages 489–492 of the notes, hands you nine instructions and a VLIW that can start three of them in every cycle. The table you will fill at the end of this lesson is 14 cycles long: 42 issue slots, of which 9 are used. The other 33 stay empty — and the paper forbids the two tricks that would fill them.

Two questions. Is 14 the best any scheduler can get out of this machine? And the machine is a single sentence: one memory unit with a latency of 3, one FP unit with 3, one integer unit with 1 (2 into a branch), one delay slot. If you could change one number in that sentence, which change would buy a cycle — and which would buy nothing at all?

Half of the first question needs no table at all. A graph of the nine instructions, weighted with their latencies, sets a floor that no number of units can push down; the distance from that floor to 14 is what this machine's units cost. You will measure it.

One loop body, one graph

Lesson 19 left the compiler of a VLIW with one job: pack independent operations into bundles without changing what the program computes. The unit it works on is the basic block.

Basic block — a straight-line sequence of instructions with one entry (no jump lands in its middle) and one exit (only its last instruction may branch). If its first instruction runs, all of them run, in order. That is what makes reordering them safe (p. 276).

The body of every loop in the exam papers is one basic block, and the BNE that closes it is its exit. Here is the January 2026 body as the notes print it (p. 489), without its dollar signs: that paper and the July 2024 one write $F2, the June 2025 paper mixes both, the June 2026 paper writes F2 — and so does this notebook.

FOR: LD    F2, VB(R6)     # I0
     FADD  F4, F2, F6     # I1
     SD    F4, VA(R7)     # I2
     LD    F4, VC(R6)     # I3
     SD    F4, VC(R7)     # I4
     FADD  F8, F8, F8     # I5
     ADDUI R6, R6, 4      # I6
     ADDUI R7, R7, 4      # I7
     BNE   R7, R8, FOR    # I8

The compiler turns the block into a dependence graph, a DAG: one node per instruction, one edge from I to J whenever J must wait for I (p. 277). The edges are the three dependences of lesson 7, and on the exam's machine each kind has its own timing rule. Write the latency on the edge: it is the number the scheduler will use.

  • RAW, the true dependence: I1 reads F2, which I0 writes. The value does not exist before the producer's latency has passed, so the consumer goes at the producer's cycle plus the latency: 3 after a load, 3 after an FP operation, 1 after an integer operation — and 2 when the consumer is the branch.
  • WAR, the anti-dependence: I3 writes F4, which I2 still has to read. Only the name is shared — another destination register would remove the edge (renaming, lesson 15), but in the exam table you only reorder, so it stays. The paper says that "it is possible to read and write at the same address at the same clock cycle", so the writer may share the cycle of its last reader — never come before it.
  • WAW, the output dependence: I1 and I3 both write F4, and the second result must land after the first. In the papers' loops this never binds: RAW and WAR edges already order every such pair.

Two facts about the branch close the rule book. The BNE is the exit, so nothing is scheduled after it. And the branch is resolved in ID, with one delay slot: the cycle after the BNE belongs to the iteration, because the next iteration cannot start before it. Fig. 1 shows the three rules where you will meet them — in the exam table itself.

Now draw all the edges of the January loop at once (Fig. 2). Read it as a set of promises: a solid arrow with a 3 says that the instruction at its head goes at least three cycles after the one at its tail; a dashed arrow says no earlier than its tail. Two things can be read off the picture (p. 276): which instructions are independent — no path joins them, so they may share a cycle if the units allow — and the longest chain, which limits everything else. I5 floats free: nobody else touches F8.

The notes' list on p. 490 misses I0 to I6: I0 reads R6 too. It never binds, because I3 reads R6 later, but a dependence table must list it.

The longest path: a floor no machine can beat

Forget the machine for a moment and give it as many units as it wants. Every instruction can then start the moment its incoming edges allow, and the block lasts exactly as long as its heaviest chain of edges. That chain is the critical path. A kitchen knows the idea: if the sauce simmers three minutes before it goes on the plate, a second cook does not make it simmer faster.

The notes compute it node by node, from the sources down (eq. 65, p. 277; the notes write "Longest Path" and "Latency" in full). Take their Example 2: seven operations, no registers, latencies 3 for a multiply, 1 for an add or subtract, 2 for the store.

\[ \text{LP}(i) = \max_{p \,\in\, \text{pred}(i)} \text{LP}(p) + \text{Lat}(i) \] \[ \text{CP} = \max_i \ \text{LP}(i) \]
\(\text{LP}(i)\)
longest path from the start to \(i\), included: the earliest cycle in which \(i\) can finish
\(\text{pred}(i)\)
the instructions with an edge into \(i\); for a source, \(\text{LP}(i) = \text{Lat}(i)\)
\(\text{Lat}(i)\)
latency of \(i\) in cycles
\(\text{CP}\)
critical path: the largest \(\text{LP}\), reached at a sink

The left panel of Fig. 3 runs it on Example 2: a = 3, b = c = 1, e = 2, d = 3 + 3 = 6, f = max(2, 1) + 2 = 4, g = max(6, 4) + 1 = 7. The critical path is 7 cycles, along a → d → g. Why is that number a floor for every machine, and not just a property of this drawing? The stepper proves it.

  1. Call \(t(i)\) the cycle in which \(i\) is issued and \(\ell(p \to i)\) the latency on an edge (0 on a dashed one). Every schedule, on any machine, must respect every edge:

    \[ t(i) \ge t(p) + \ell(p \to i) . \]

  2. Follow a path \(p_0 \to p_1 \to \dots \to p_k\) and add its inequalities: the intermediate terms cancel.

    \[ t(p_k) \ge t(p_0) + \textstyle\sum_j \ell_j . \]

  3. The first instruction issues in cycle 1 at the earliest, and the last one still needs its own latency: the block lasts at least

    \[ 1 + \textstyle\sum_j \ell_j + \text{Lat}(p_k) - 1 , \]

    which is the weight of the path as eq. 65 counts it.

  4. This holds for every path, so for the heaviest one: any schedule lasts at least CP cycles. Extra units only remove reasons to wait; they remove no edge.

  5. With unlimited units each instruction can go exactly at \(\max_p [\,t(p) + \ell(p \to i)\,]\). That schedule meets the bound, so the bound is tight: with unlimited units the block takes exactly CP cycles.

The schedule of the last step has a name: ASAP, as soon as possible (p. 279). It schedules every operation in the first cycle its operands allow and ignores resources — the notes compare it to planning a trip as if every traffic light were green (p. 280). On a real machine it is rarely a legal schedule — it may ask for more units than exist — but it is precious as a measuring stick: it gives the lower bound, the earliest cycle of every instruction and, early in the compiler, a first estimate of how much parallelism there is to exploit. Here is Example 2 under ASAP, with the unit columns of the notes; the ready list holds the instructions whose operands are available in that cycle.

In cycle 3 the store f occupies only the L/S column: ALU1 is empty. In cycle 6 the MUL column holds d.

A store uses the load/store unit and nothing else — the notes' own Example 3 has no such entry. And d is issued in cycle 4 with latency 3, so — as a does in cycles 1–3 — it fills the MUL column in cycles 4, 5 and 6: g can start in cycle 7 only because d finishes in 6, exactly as the ready list on the same page says.

The scheduler of the next section needs the opposite number. Not "how long did it take to reach this instruction", but "how long does the graph still run after it". That is the priority of list scheduling: the longest path from the instruction to a sink, computed backwards, from the sinks up (p. 281).

\[ \text{prio}(i) = \text{Lat}(i) + \max_{s \,\in\, \text{succ}(i)} \text{prio}(s) \]
\(\text{succ}(i)\)
the instructions with an edge out of \(i\); for a sink, \(\text{prio}(i) = \text{Lat}(i)\)
\(\text{prio}(i)\)
the cycles the block still needs from the issue of \(i\) to its end; the higher, the more urgent

"One way to assign this priority is using the Longest Path to Sink (from ASAP): instructions deeper in the graph (closer to the end) are more urgent."

The longest path to a sink is computed backwards from the sinks, not from the ASAP schedule, which runs forwards from the sources. And the urgent instructions are those with the longest path still ahead of them — usually near the top of the graph; an instruction close to the end has little left and a low priority.

Fig. 3 puts the two numbers side by side: they differ at every node, and they rank differently — ASAP's largest number sits on g, the priority's on a. The notes' own Example 3 serves a (priority 7) in cycle 1 and g (priority 1) last. Page 281 repeats the slip: "Nodes closer to the end of the graph are more critical (less slack)".

The January floor by hand: the edge weights 3, 3, 0, 3, 0, 2 and the branch's 2 add up to 13.

On the exam machine one detail changes: the latency belongs to the edge, not to the node. An ADDUI that feeds a store counts 1; the same ADDUI feeding the BNE counts 2. The branch itself weighs 2 — its own cycle plus the delay slot — and every other instruction must be issued no later than the branch. With these weights, I0 of the January loop gets priority 13: the floor of the puzzle, the highlighted chain of Fig. 2.

List scheduling, one cycle at a time

A real machine has a fixed number of units, and that is all list scheduling adds to ASAP (p. 281). In every cycle it collects the ready set — the instructions whose operands are available in that cycle — and hands the free units to the most urgent ones. The bookkeeping of who occupies which unit in which cycle is the resource reservation table.

  1. Build the dependence graph and compute every priority.
  2. Start at cycle 1. An instruction is ready when every predecessor is placed early enough: RAW at least \(\ell\) cycles before, WAR in this cycle or before.
  3. While a unit is free and something ready fits it, take the highest-priority instruction (ties: program order) and place it in this cycle. Placing it may make a WAR successor ready in the same cycle.
  4. Move to the next cycle; repeat until every instruction is placed.

The algorithm is greedy: it never revisits a choice, so it is a heuristic for a problem that is hard in general, not an optimiser. With the longest-path priority it finds the shortest schedule on most graphs of exam size — the June 2025 loop of the exam questions is the exception that shows why it is called a heuristic. Here is the notes' Example 3: the graph of Example 2 on one ALU, one L/S unit and one MUL.

Cycle 1: a takes the MUL; b (5) beats c (4) for the only ALU. Cycle 2: c and e tie at 4 and program order picks c. Cycle 4: d and f each have a unit of their own. The VLIW code, one bundle per row, reads {b, a}, {c}, {e}, {f, d}, two empty bundles, {g}: 7 operations in 21 slots. The schedule is 7 cycles long — the critical path. One ALU instead of two cost nothing, because the ALU operations have slack while the chain through the MUL is critical.

Resource Reservation Table, rows 3 to 5: "3 | e | f | a", "4 | | f | d", "5 | | | d" (columns ALU1, L/S, MUL).

f occupies the L/S unit in cycles 4 and 5, not 3 and 4.

f needs the result of e, issued in cycle 3 with latency 1, so it cannot be issued before cycle 4 — which is what the Ready List ("4 | d, f") and the VLIW Code table on the same page say. The reservation table contradicts both.

The exam table: conventions, a forced schedule, four numbers

Exercise 2 of the papers of July 2024, June 2025 and June 2026 is this algorithm on paper, worth 5 points: "complete the following table by using the list-based scheduling", one row per cycle, one column per unit — Memory Unit, Floating Point Unit, Integer Unit — with the first instruction already written in C1. The texts give the machine; the rest is the convention of the solutions worked out in the notes (pp. 491–492 and 561), which this notebook follows. Keep all of it on your formula sheet:

  1. Units are fully pipelined: each takes one new instruction per cycle, and the three units are the three issue slots of a bundle.
  2. RAW: the consumer goes at the producer's cycle + 3 after a load or an FP operation, + 1 after an integer operation, + 2 when the consumer is the branch.
  3. WAR: the writer may share the cycle of the last reader of its register, never come before it. WAW: the second writer's result lands later.
  4. The BNE is the last instruction. The row after it is the delay slot: write "(delay slot)", count it, put nothing in it.
  5. No NOPs are written: an empty cell stays empty. No unrolling, no software pipelining, no change of offsets or indexes: you only reorder.
  6. The answers: the critical path for a single iteration is the number of cycles of your table, delay slot included; CPI = cycles / instructions; FP ops per cycle = FP operations / cycles; code efficiency = instructions / (cycles × issue width).
Why two cycles from an integer operation to the branch, but one to anything else?

The paper says the branch is "solved in ID stage". An ordinary consumer needs its operand when it reaches EX; the branch needs it one stage earlier, in ID. So the result of the ADDUI, usable by an ordinary instruction issued one cycle later, is usable by the branch only one cycle after that. The same reasoning explains the delay slot: by the time the branch is decided in ID, the next bundle has already been fetched, and it executes — the solutions in the notes keep that slot empty and count it.

Now the January 2026 loop on its machine. The stepper places the instructions in the order list scheduling meets them; every placement on the chain is forced.

  1. C1. Ready: I0 (priority 13) and I5 (2). I6 is not ready although nothing feeds it a value: it overwrites R6, which I3 has not read yet. I0 takes the Memory Unit, I5 the FP unit.

  2. C4. I1 needs F2 from the load: C1 + 3. Nothing else is ready in C2 and C3, so they stay empty.

  3. C7. I2 needs F4 from I1: C4 + 3. I3 is ready in C7 too — its WAR with I2 allows the same cycle — but the single Memory Unit is taken.

  4. C8. I3 takes the Memory Unit; I6, whose last reader of R6 is I3, joins it in the Integer Unit. C9 and C10 wait for the load.

  5. C11. I4 stores the F4 just loaded: C8 + 3. I7 shares the cycle: I4 is the last reader of R7.

  6. C13. The BNE needs R7 from I7: C11 + 2. C14 is the delay slot. The iteration is 14 cycles long.

Two instructions have freedom, because nothing waits for them: I5 may sit in any free FP cell up to C13, and I6 in C8, C9, C10 or C12. Moving them changes no number. The paper's four questions, answered as you would write them:

\[ \text{critical path} = 14 \ \text{cycles} \] \[ \text{FP ops per cycle} = \tfrac{2}{14} \approx 0.14 \] \[ \text{code efficiency} = \tfrac{9}{14 \times 3} = \tfrac{9}{42} \approx 0.21 \] \[ \text{CPI} = \tfrac{14}{9} \approx 1.56 \]

Back to the puzzle. The floor of the January loop is 13 cycles: the chain of Fig. 2, which ASAP reaches when I2 and I3 share C7 (Fig. 4, left). The paper's machine has one Memory Unit, so I3 slips to C8 and the whole tail with it: 14 (right). And 14 is the best this machine allows. I3 may not go before I2 (WAR on F4) nor with it (one memory unit), so it lands in C8 at the earliest, and every later step of the chain is forced.

Now change one number of the machine sentence. A second Memory Unit gives 13: the gap closes, and nothing more can be gained by adding units. A memory latency of 2 gives 12, an FP latency of 2 or an integer-to-branch latency of 1 give 13: each of them shortens an edge of the chain, so it moves the floor itself. A second FP unit or a second integer unit gives nothing: I5 and the two ADDUI instructions never wait for their unit. Units can only close the distance to the floor; latencies move the floor.

Local and global scheduling: the map of the next lesson

Everything so far was local scheduling: it works inside one basic block, where control flow is a straight line and the compiler knows every instruction and every dependence (p. 283). Its goal is to fill the slots with real instructions instead of NOPs. Its limit is the block itself: instructions cannot cross the block's boundaries, so the parallelism is bounded by the block's size and its chains — nine instructions, one long chain, 33 empty slots.

Global scheduling works across blocks — across branches, across loop boundaries, along whole paths of control flow. It finds more parallelism and hides long latencies, at a price: control dependences must be preserved too, moves that guess wrong need compensation code, and the compiler becomes more complex and more expensive to build. ASAP and list scheduling sit under all four: each technique reshapes the code, and then a list scheduler fills the bundles. The exam's Exercise 2 forbids the first two by name — "do NOT introduce any software pipelining, loop unrolling and modifications to loop indexes" — so the exercise tests the list scheduler alone. Exam-style questions

Explain the main concepts and benefits of each one of the following techniques used by a compiler to schedule code statically for a VLIW processor:

  1. Basic block
  2. Dependence graph
  3. Critical path
  4. ASAP scheduling
  5. List-based scheduling
  6. Priority based on the longest path to the sink
  7. Resource reservation table
  8. Local versus global scheduling
Show model answer
  1. A straight-line sequence of instructions with one entry and one exit (only the last instruction may branch). All its instructions execute together, so the compiler can reorder them freely as long as the data dependences hold. It is the unit of local scheduling; its limit is its size — a few instructions between branches.
  2. A DAG with one node per instruction and one edge per dependence: RAW (true), WAR and WAW (name dependences). RAW edges carry the producer's latency. It shows what may run in parallel (unconnected nodes) and what must wait, and it is the input of every scheduler.
  3. The longest latency-weighted path from a source to a sink, LP(i) = max LP(p) + Lat(i). It is the minimum execution time of the block even with unlimited resources: no schedule can beat it. In the exam's VLIW exercise, "critical path for a single iteration" is answered with the length of the scheduled iteration, delay slot included.
  4. Each operation is scheduled in the first cycle in which its operands are available, ignoring resources. It gives the lower bound (= the critical path) and the earliest cycle of every operation; it is not a valid schedule on a real machine, because it can use more units than exist.
  5. ASAP plus resource constraints: in every cycle the ready instructions (operands available) are sorted by priority and the highest ones take the free units. It is greedy and fast, close to optimal on small graphs, and it is what a VLIW compiler — and the exam table — uses.
  6. The priority of an instruction is the longest path from it to the end of the block, computed backwards from the sinks. Instructions with a long chain still ahead go first; instructions with slack fill the holes; ties are broken in program order.
  7. A table of cycles × functional units recording which unit is busy in which cycle. The scheduler checks it before placing an instruction, so no unit is oversubscribed (with fully pipelined units: one new instruction per unit per cycle). Read row by row, it gives the bundles.
  8. Local scheduling works inside one basic block (ASAP, list scheduling; unrolling and software pipelining reshape a loop body); it is simple but limited by the block. Global scheduling moves instructions across branches (trace and superblock scheduling): more ILP, at the cost of compensation code and a more complex compiler.

A load is in C3 on a Memory Unit with latency 3. In which cycle can the FADD that uses its result go, at the earliest?

  • C6 Producer's cycle + latency: 3 + 3. C4 and C5 stay empty — two empty rows, not three.
  • C7 That would be three empty rows after the load: latency 3 means the consumer goes three rows below, not three rows plus one.
  • C4 The next cycle is enough only after an integer operation (latency 1).
  • C3 The same cycle is allowed for a WAR, never for a RAW: the value does not exist yet.

In C1 of the January 2026 loop, the Integer Unit is free and ADDUI R6, R6, 4 has no RAW predecessor. Why is it not placed there?

  • It would overwrite R6 before LD F4, VC(R6) reads it A WAR: the writer may share the cycle of its last reader, but the reader I3 is placed only in C8.
  • It needs F2 from the first load ADDUI reads only R6; the edge from I0 is a WAR on R6, not a RAW on F2.
  • The Integer Unit is reserved for the branch The unit takes any integer instruction in any cycle; the BNE simply has to be the last one.
  • Nothing prevents it: any free slot of its unit will do The WAR does: I3 reads R6 in C8, so the ADDUI goes in C8 or later. Moving it up anyway would need new offsets, and the paper forbids "modifications to loop indexes".

Which statements about the critical path are true? Select all that apply.

  • No schedule of the block, on any machine, is shorter than it Every path is a chain of inequalities t(i) ≥ t(p) + ℓ that no unit removes.
  • ASAP reaches it when units are unlimited ASAP puts every instruction at the earliest cycle its edges allow, which is exactly the bound.
  • In the exam's VLIW table, "critical path for a single iteration" is answered with the length of the table, delay slot included That is the convention of the notes' solutions: 14 for January 2026, where the graph's own longest path is 13.
  • It counts instructions, not cycles It is weighted by latencies: LD → FADD weighs 6 cycles with two instructions.
  • Adding a unit of the right kind always shortens it Units never appear in the longest path; they only bring a real schedule closer to it.

In the notes' Example 3, one ALU instead of two costs nothing: the schedule stays at 7 cycles. Why?

  • The ALU operations have slack: the critical path runs through the MUL b, c and e can be spread over cycles 1–3 without delaying d or g; make the MUL faster and the ALU becomes the bottleneck.
  • ASAP ignores resources True of ASAP, but Example 3 is a list schedule that respects one ALU.
  • List scheduling always reaches the critical path Not always: with the MUL latency at 1, one ALU gives 6 cycles against a critical path of 5.
  • The store also runs on the ALU The store uses only the L/S unit — that is one of the corrections of p. 280.

Key take-aways

  • A basic block — one entry, one exit — becomes a dependence graph: RAW edges carry the producer's latency, WAR edges allow the same cycle, WAW edges order the two writes.
  • The longest path of the graph (eq. 65) is the critical path: a floor for every schedule on every machine, reached by ASAP when units are unlimited.
  • List scheduling is ASAP plus units: in every cycle the ready set's most urgent instructions take the free units, and urgency is the longest path to the end, computed backwards.
  • The exam table follows fixed conventions: latencies 3 / 3 / 1 and 2 into the branch, WAR in the same cycle, the BNE last, the delay slot counted and empty, no NOPs, no unrolling or software pipelining.
  • Its four numbers for January 2026: critical path 14 cycles, FP ops per cycle 2/14 ≈ 0.14, code efficiency 9/42 ≈ 0.21, CPI 14/9 ≈ 1.56; February 2025: 13 cycles and 12/39 = 30.77 %.
  • Units can only close the gap to the floor (January: 14 → 13 with a second memory unit); latencies move the floor itself.
