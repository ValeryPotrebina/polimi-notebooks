Из чего складывается оценка

У оценки две части, теоретическая и практическая. Итог — их сумма.

  • Письменный экзамен по всей программе курса: до 26 баллов из 30. До 13 из них выставляет проф. Matteucci, до 13 — проф. Boracchi.
  • Домашний проект — одно соревнование по программированию (coding challenge): до 6 баллов. Их дают за решение, место в рейтинге и отчёт, а потом умножают на коэффициент \(\alpha\).
  • Итог: сумма двух оценок, максимум 32 из 30.
\[ S = W + \alpha \cdot C \]

Как проходит письменный экзамен

  • Экзамен цифровой, на Moodle: платформа remoteexam.polimi.it, вход через квиз на WeBeep.
  • Нужен свой ноутбук и Safe Exam Browser (SEB): он обязателен. На Linux SEB не работает — понадобится ноутбук с Windows или Mac.

Как тренироваться

Работай в условиях экзамена: без конспекта и шпаргалки, с непрограммируемым калькулятором. Сначала ответ, потом проверка.

  • Open — развёрнутый ответ. Напиши его на бумаге или в редакторе по-английски: вопросы и модельные ответы здесь на английском, как слайды курса. Потом открой Show model answer и сравни по пунктам.
  • Numeric — число. Посчитай, введи результат и нажми Check. Подходят записи 0.75, 0,75 и 3/4; ответ засчитывается с небольшим допуском на округление.
  • Multiple choice — выбор вариантов. Если верный вариант один, ответ проверяется сразу по клику. Если их несколько, под вариантами есть кнопка Check: отметь все верные и нажми её. После проверки под вариантами появятся пояснения.

Под каждым модельным ответом есть комментарий по-русски: что именно проверяют и где обычно теряют баллы. Ссылка «открыть в уроке» над рамкой ведёт к этому же вопросу в уроке. Туда стоит вернуться, если ответ не сложился: комментарии иногда ссылаются на рисунок, таблицу или виджет урока.

Ответы на этой странице не сохраняются: после перезагрузки все вопросы снова чистые. Это удобно, чтобы пройти их заново через неделю.

State Mitchell's definition of what it means for a computer program to learn. Then give T, E and P for (a) an email spam filter and (b) a chess program that trains by playing against itself.

Show model answer

A computer program learns from experience E with respect to a class of tasks T and a performance measure P if its performance at the tasks in T, as measured by P, improves with experience E.

(a) T: classify an email as spam or not spam. E: a set of emails labelled with both classes. P: accuracy (or error rate, or F1) on emails not used for training.

(b) T: play chess. E: the games played against itself. P: the fraction of games won.

Потерять балл легче всего, написав в (b) T: win games.

A spam filter is evaluated on 200 unseen emails (positive class = spam): TP = 40, FP = 10, FN = 5, TN = 145. Compute the F1 score. Give three decimals.

Show model answer

Precision \(= 40 / (40 + 10) = 0.800\). Recall \(= 40 / (40 + 5) = 0.889\).

\[ F_1 = \frac{2 \cdot 0.800 \cdot 0.889}{0.800 + 0.889} = 0.842 \]

Directly from the counts: \(F_1 = \frac{2 \cdot 40}{2 \cdot 40 + 10 + 5} = \frac{80}{95}\). For comparison, accuracy \(= 185 / 200 = 0.925\).

Типичные потери: посчитать accuracy вместо F1 или перепутать FP и FN местами. Во втором случае F1 не изменится, формула симметрична по FP и FN, но precision и recall поменяются ролями, и ответ на вопрос о любой из них будет неверным. Считай по клеткам и не округляй промежуточные числа.

Only 5% of the emails in a mailbox are spam. Which is more informative for evaluating a spam filter, accuracy or F1? Explain why.

Show model answer

F1. With strong class imbalance accuracy is dominated by the majority class: a filter that always answers "not spam" reaches 95% accuracy while detecting no spam at all. F1 is the harmonic mean of precision and recall of the positive (spam) class and does not count true negatives; for that trivial filter recall is 0, so F1 is 0.

Ответ без примера «всегда отвечает not spam — и получает 95 %» выглядит заученным. Одно предложение с этим числом показывает, что механизм понят.

Урок 1 · Что значит «машина учится» · вопрос 6 из 6 · открыть в уроке

A spam filter reaches 100% accuracy on the emails it was trained on. Explain why this does not show that it has learned, and how its performance should be measured instead. Refer to empirical and expected risk.

Show model answer

Accuracy on the training emails is one minus the empirical risk, i.e. the average loss over the N examples that were used to choose the parameters. What we care about is the expected risk: the average loss over the unknown data distribution, i.e. over new emails.

Because the parameters were fitted to those N examples, the empirical risk is an optimistically biased estimate of the expected risk. A lookup table that memorises the training set has zero empirical risk but answers new emails at chance level.

Performance must therefore be estimated on data not used for training (a test set). By Mitchell's definition the program has learned if this performance improves as its experience grows.

Полный балл — за пару терминов empirical / expected risk, за слово optimistic (или biased) и за контрпример с таблицей. Фраза «потому что это overfitting» без объяснения механизма тянет на половину.

Using the notation \(D = x_1, \dots, x_N\), state what the experience contains in supervised, unsupervised and reinforcement learning. Then explain why a reward is not the same thing as a label.

Show model answer

Supervised: the data \(D\) come with the desired outputs \(t_1, \dots, t_N\); the learner must produce the correct output for a new input. Unsupervised: only \(D\) is given; the learner exploits regularities in \(D\) to build a representation to be used for reasoning or prediction (e.g. clusters). Reinforcement: the learner produces actions \(a_1, \dots, a_N\) that affect the environment and receives rewards \(r_1, \dots, r_N\); it learns to act so as to maximise rewards in the long term.

A label is instructive feedback: it states the correct output for an input, whatever the learner answered. A reward is evaluative feedback: it only scores the action that was actually taken, says nothing about the other actions, and may arrive many steps later. Moreover, in reinforcement learning the data depend on the learner's own behaviour, while a supervised data set is fixed.

В ответе должны быть две вещи: формулировки слайда 5 лекции 1 (обозначения \(t_n\), \(a_n\), \(r_n\) и слова «in the long term») и различие между оценкой и инструкцией. Ответ «reward is a kind of label» вторую часть не закрывает.

What is a Markov Decision Process? Why must the discount factor be strictly smaller than 1 in an infinite-horizon task?

Show model answer

An MDP is a tuple \((\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, R, \gamma)\): a set of states, a set of actions, the transition probabilities \(P(s' \mid s, a)\), the reward function \(R(s, a)\) and the discount factor \(\gamma\). It satisfies the Markov property: the next state depends only on the current state and action, not on the earlier history.

If the rewards are bounded, \(\abs{r_t} \le R_{\max}\), then

\[ \abs{G} \le R_{\max} \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^{t} = \frac{R_{\max}}{1 - \gamma} \]

which is finite only for \(\gamma \lt 1\). With \(\gamma = 1\) an infinite sum of rewards can diverge (e.g. \(r_t = 1\) at every step), so the returns of different policies could not be compared. \(\gamma = 1\) is acceptable only for episodic tasks of finite length.

Первая половина — на знание определения: пять элементов и свойство Маркова. Если написать «task, experience, performance», как в конспекте, это ответ на другой вопрос. Во второй половине ждут геометрическую прогрессию.

In the traditional pipeline "hand-crafted features + learned classifier", the chosen features can make a classification task easy, complex or impossible. Describe the three cases and say, for each, which part of the pipeline has to change.

Show model answer

Easy: in the chosen feature space the classes are linearly separable, so a simple (linear) classifier is enough; nothing has to change.

Complex: the classes do not overlap, but the boundary between them is non-linear (e.g. one class surrounded by the other). The features are still informative; a more flexible classifier is needed (nearest neighbours, RBF SVM, trees), or equivalently a non-linear change of representation.

Impossible: the classes overlap in feature space, in the extreme case different-class samples have identical feature vectors. The classifier only sees the features, so no classifier can separate them; the features must be changed. This happens when we do not know which features to extract, and it is what motivates learning the features from data.

Проверяют, что ты различаешь две поломки: кривую границу (лечит классификатор) и отсутствие границы (лечат признаки). Ответ в духе конспекта («простые признаки, сложные признаки») расходится со слайдами 19–23 лекции 1: там сложным названо решение, а не признаки. Хорошо звучит фраза профессора: "this happens if you do not know which features to extract".

Урок 3 · Всё дело в признаках · вопрос 2 из 6 · открыть в уроке

Sketch the object-recognition pipeline used between 2006 and 2012. For each stage say whether it is fixed, learned without labels or learned with labels. What does deep learning change, and why can a better classifier not compensate for bad features?

Show model answer

Image → SIFT / HoG (low-level features, fixed, hand-designed) → K-means or sparse coding (mid-level features, unsupervised learning) → pooling (fixed) → classifier, e.g. an SVM (supervised learning) → label.

Only the last stage is trained with the labels; the error made at the output never reaches the first stages, so the features are not optimised for the task.

Deep learning replaces the fixed and unsupervised stages with layers of learned features that are trained jointly with the classifier by backpropagation, directly from raw data. The result is a hierarchical representation optimised for the task, which also makes the final classifier easier to learn.

A classifier receives only the feature vector. Information about the class that the features discarded cannot be recovered: if samples of different classes are mapped to the same features, every classifier gives them the same answer.

Блок Pooling часто забывают; на слайде под ним подписи нет, но обучаемых параметров в нём тоже нет, так что fixed — честный ответ. Для речи ответ устроен так же: MFCC → Mixture of Gaussian → classifier.

Two classes, A and B, are described by two features. Урок 5 · Машина, которая сама крутит ручки · вопрос 1 из 6 · открыть в уроке

Write the mathematical model of an artificial neuron with a firing threshold \(b\). Then show how the threshold can be represented as a weight and say why this is convenient.

Show model answer

The neuron computes a weighted sum of its \(I\) inputs, subtracts the threshold and applies an activation function \(h_j\) (a step: 1 if its argument is positive, 0 otherwise):

\[ h_j(\vb{x} \mid \vb{w}, b) = h_j\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{I} w_i x_i - b\Big) \]

Add a constant input \(x_0 = 1\) with weight \(w_0 = -b\). Then \(-b = w_0 x_0\) is simply the \(i = 0\) term of the sum:

\[ h_j\Big(\sum_{i=0}^{I} w_i x_i\Big) = h_j(\vb{w}\T\vb{x}) \]

with \(\vb{x} = (1, x_1, \dots, x_I)\) and \(\vb{w} = (w_0, w_1, \dots, w_I)\). The weight \(w_0\) is the bias. It is convenient because all \(I + 1\) parameters sit in one vector, the sum is always compared with zero, and the same learning rule updates the bias like any other weight.

В полном ответе три вещи: строка с тремя равенствами, явные слова «\(x_0 = 1\), \(w_0 = -b\)» (без них второе равенство неверно) и знак. Фраза «the bias is the threshold» без минуса — ошибка: это два числа с противоположными знаками.

Computers were good at doing exactly what they were programmed to do and at very fast arithmetic. Researchers also wanted machines that could (i) interact with noisy data or directly with the environment, (ii) be massively parallel and fault tolerant, (iii) adapt to circumstances.

The output depends only on the sign of \(a = w_0 + \vb{w}\T\vb{x}\), i.e. on which side of the hyperplane \(w_0 + \vb{w}\T\vb{x} = 0\) the input lies. The boundary is linear (a line in 2D: \(x_2 = -\tfrac{w_0}{w_2} - \tfrac{w_1}{w_2}x_1\)), although the output itself is a step.

Multiplying everything by \(-1\) leaves the equation \(a = 0\), hence the boundary, unchanged, but flips the sign of \(a\) everywhere: the two regions swap (AND becomes NAND).

For every weight \(w_i\), \(i = 0, \dots, I\):

\[ w_i^{k+1} = w_i^{k} + \Delta w_i^{k}, \qquad \Delta w_i^{k} = \eta\, x_i^{k}\, t^{k} \]

\(\eta\) is the learning rate, \(x_i^{k}\) is the \(i\)-th input of the sample presented at step \(k\) (with \(x_0 = 1\), so the bias \(w_0\) changes by \(\eta\, t^{k}\)), and \(t^{k} \in \{-1, +1\}\) is the desired output. The weights start from a random initialisation. Samples are presented one at a time (online), and the weights are changed only if the sample is not correctly predicted, i.e. \(\mathrm{Sign}(\vb{w}\T\vb{x}) \ne t\). The records are cycled until a whole pass produces no error.

Проверяют четыре вещи: формулу, слово online, условие «только если предсказано неверно» и критерий остановки. Чаще всего теряют третье, а без него это другой алгоритм.

Does Hebbian learning of a perceptron always terminate? When it terminates, is the final weight vector always the same? Justify both answers.

Show model answer

It terminates after a finite number of updates if and only if the training set is linearly separable (perceptron convergence theorem). Урок 8 · Четыре точки, остановившие науку · вопрос 5 из 6 · открыть в уроке

Explain why Hebbian learning cannot be used to train a Multi Layer Perceptron.

Show model answer

The Hebbian rule \(\Delta w_i = \eta\, x_i\, t\) corrects the weights of a neuron using the desired output \(t\) of that neuron. The training set provides targets \(t_n\) only for the output neurons. For hidden neurons no desired output is known, so the rule cannot be applied to them: we do not know which hidden unit is responsible for an output error (credit assignment).

In addition, the step activation is not differentiable (its derivative is zero almost everywhere), so the output error carries no gradient information about the hidden weights. This is why Feed Forward Neural Networks use differentiable activation functions and are trained by gradient descent with backpropagation.

Ключевая фраза — «no target for hidden units». Про недифференцируемую ступеньку стоит добавить вторым предложением: это мостик к слайду 17 лекции 2.

With a linear hidden activation \(\vb{h} = W^{(1)}\vb{x} + \vb{b}^{(1)}\), so the input of the output layer is

\[ W^{(2)}\big(W^{(1)}\vb{x} + \vb{b}^{(1)}\big) + \vb{b}^{(2)} \] \[ = \big(W^{(2)}W^{(1)}\big)\vb{x} + \big(W^{(2)}\vb{b}^{(1)} + \vb{b}^{(2)}\big) \] \[ = W\vb{x} + \vb{b} . \]

The step function is non-linear (a network of step units with one hidden layer can represent XOR), but its derivative is zero everywhere except at the jump, where it does not exist: the gradient carries no information.

Sigmoid: range \((0, 1)\), \(g' = g\,(1 - g) \le 0.25\), outputs always positive. Tanh: range \((-1, 1)\), \(g' = 1 - g^2 \le 1\), an odd function with zero-centred outputs. They are the same curve up to scaling: \(\tanh(a) = 2\,\sigma(2a) - 1\).

It does not solve the vanishing gradient problem. Tanh also saturates for large \(\abs{a}\), even sooner than the sigmoid, and there \(g'(a) \approx 0\). The gradient of the early layers is a product of many such factors and still vanishes in deep networks.

State the universal approximation theorem for feed-forward neural networks. What does it guarantee and what does it not guarantee? Why are deep networks used in practice although one hidden layer is enough in theory?

Show model answer

A feed-forward neural network with a single hidden layer of S-shaped units (sigmoid, tanh) and a linear output can approximate any continuous (more generally, measurable) function on a compact set to any desired degree of accuracy, provided it has enough hidden units (Hornik, 1991). Formally, for continuous \(f\) on a compact \(K\) and every \(\varepsilon \gt 0\) there exist \(J\) and weights \(w\) such that

\[ \abs{f(x) - g(x|w)} \lt \varepsilon \quad \forall x \in K . \]

It is an existence result about what the model can represent. It does not guarantee that (i) a learning algorithm can find the necessary weights; (ii) the number of hidden units is reasonable: in the worst case it is exponential, so the layer may be infeasibly large; (iii) the trained network generalizes from a finite, noisy training set. Nothing is claimed outside the compact set.

Deep networks can represent many functions with far fewer units than one wide layer, because each layer reuses the features built by the previous ones. This is why depth is preferred in practice.

Проверяют три вещи. Формулировку: «single hidden layer», «S-shaped», «compact set», «any accuracy». Слово «existence». И три «но» со слайда 20 лекции 2, желательно его же словами. Самая частая потеря баллов — написать «a network can learn any function»: теорема не про обучение.

Write the gradient used by batch, stochastic and mini-batch gradient descent, and state the trade-off between them.

Show model answer

Batch:

\[ \pd{E(w)}{w} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n}^{N} \pd{E(x_n, w)}{w} \]

Stochastic:

\[ \pd{E(w)}{w} \approx \pd{E(x_n, w)}{w} \]

Mini-batch:

\[ \pd{E(w)}{w} \approx \frac{1}{M} \sum_{n \in \text{Minibatch}}^{M \lt N} \pd{E(x_n, w)}{w} \]

Batch uses all \(N\) samples for each update: the gradient is exact but each step is expensive. SGD uses a single sample: an unbiased estimate with high variance, very cheap. Mini-batch uses a subset of \(M \lt N\) samples: a good trade-off between variance and computation.

(a) Perturbing the weights (finite differences) needs one extra forward pass per weight, i.e. \(W + 1\) passes for \(W\) weights, and gives only an approximation. The cost is one forward plus one backward pass, and the gradient is exact.

(c) Every backward step multiplies the error signal by the derivative of an activation function, \(g'(a)\) or \(h'(a)\). A step function has zero derivative almost everywhere, so no error signal would reach the earlier weights.

Проверяют три шага: ортогональность через разность двух точек, единичную нормаль и подстановку \(\vb{w}\T\vb{x}_0 = -w_0\). Слово «proportional» важно: без деления на \(\norm{\vb{w}}\) это не расстояние.

Урок 16 · Забор со стрелкой · вопрос 3 из 6 · открыть в уроке

Derive the perceptron learning rule as stochastic gradient descent on a suitable error function. Which learning rule seen earlier in the course do you obtain?

Show model answer

Code the targets as \(t_i \in \{+1, -1\}\). A point is misclassified iff \(t_i(\vb{w}\T\vb{x}_i + w_0) \lt 0\). Let \(M\) be the set of misclassified points. The error function is

\[ D(\vb{w}, w_0) = -\sum_{i \in M} t_i\,(\vb{w}\T\vb{x}_i + w_0) \]

It is non-negative and proportional to the distance of the misclassified points from the decision boundary. Its gradients are

\[ \pd{D}{\vb{w}} = -\sum_{i \in M} t_i\,\vb{x}_i, \qquad \pd{D}{w_0} = -\sum_{i \in M} t_i \]

Stochastic gradient descent uses one misclassified point at a time:

\[ \vb{w}^{k+1} = \vb{w}^{k} + \eta\,t_i\,\vb{x}_i, \qquad w_0^{k+1} = w_0^{k} + \eta\,t_i \]

With \(x_0 = 1\) the bias follows the same rule, so every weight changes by \(\Delta w_j = \eta\,x_j\,t\), applied only to wrongly predicted samples. This is the Hebbian learning rule: Hebbian learning implements stochastic gradient descent.

Полный балл — это четыре вещи: кодировка \(\pm 1\) и условие ошибки, формула \(D\) с множеством \(M\), оба градиента, шаг по одной точке с выводом «это Hebbian learning». Чаще всего теряют знак: в \(D\) минус, в градиенте минус, в правиле плюс.

Урок 16 · Забор со стрелкой · вопрос 5 из 6 · открыть в уроке

The Hebbian rule is run by cycling through the records and fixing those which are not correctly predicted. (a) Does the procedure always converge? (b) Does it always converge to the same set of weights? Justify both answers.

Show model answer

(a) No. It converges in a finite number of updates if and only if the data are linearly separable (perceptron convergence theorem). Otherwise the set of misclassified points is never empty and the weights keep changing forever: for XOR with \(\vb{w} = [0\ 0\ 0]\) and \(\eta = 0.5\) the weights return to zero after four updates and the cycle repeats.

(b) No. Every separating hyperplane is a valid stopping point, and the one reached depends on the initial weights and on the order of the records (and on \(\eta\) when the initial weights are not zero). Define underfitting and overfitting. How do you recognize each of them from the training error and the error on unseen data?

Show model answer

Underfitting: the model is too simple (or not trained enough) to capture the structure of the data. Both the training error and the error on unseen data are high.

Overfitting: the model is too complex for the available data: it fits the random noise of the training set in addition to the underlying pattern, so it does not generalize. The training error is very low, while the error on unseen data is much higher.

As model complexity grows, the training error keeps decreasing, whereas the error on unseen data first decreases and then increases. The best complexity is at the minimum of the error on unseen (validation) data.

Проверяют три вещи: в каждом определении названы обе ошибки; в определении overfitting есть слово noise; сказано, что диагноз требует данных, не участвовавших в обучении. Ответ «overfitting — когда ошибка на тесте большая» — половина балла: то же верно для underfitting. Формулировки профессора (лекция 3, слайд 3) стоит помнить дословно: "Too simple models Underfit the data ..." и "Too complex models Overfit the data and do not Generalize".

Урок 17 · Бритва Оккама · вопрос 2 из 7 · открыть в уроке

State the inductive hypothesis on which learning from examples relies. Under which conditions can it be trusted, and what makes it fail?

Show model answer

A solution approximating the target function over a sufficiently large set of training examples will also approximate it over unobserved examples. In terms of errors: with enough training examples, a small training error implies a small expected (true) error, \(\hat E_{\text{train}} \approx E\).

It can be trusted when (i) training examples and unobserved examples are i.i.d. samples from the same distribution, (ii) the training set is sufficiently large compared with the complexity of the model, and (iii) the model captures the underlying structure rather than the noise.

It fails under distribution shift, with a small dataset, and with a model that is too complex for the data. The weights are chosen to minimize the error on those very training points, so the training error is an optimistic estimate of the true error, and the gap grows with model complexity: overfitting.

Баллы — за само утверждение и за условия. Первая фраза ответа — дословно со слайда (лекция 3, слайд 3); её опорные слова — target function, sufficiently large set, unobserved examples. Не называй это inductive bias: так делает конспект (стр. 128 и 130), а на слайде стоит только Inductive Hypothesis. Inductive bias — другое понятие: предпочтения самого алгоритма, например бритва Оккама.

Урок 17 · Бритва Оккама · вопрос 3 из 7 · открыть в уроке

A feed-forward neural network with one hidden layer is a universal approximator. Explain why this does not guarantee good generalization, and what Ockham's razor suggests.

Show model answer

The theorem is an existence statement about what the network can represent: with enough hidden units it can approximate any continuous (more generally, measurable) function on a compact set to any desired degree of accuracy (Hornik, 1991). A single layer can do it, but it doesn't mean we can find the necessary weights, an exponential number of hidden units may be required, and it might be useless in practice if it does not generalize. The theorem says nothing about what is learned from a finite, noisy training set.

A model that can represent anything can also represent the noise in the training targets, so a very low training error can be reached without learning the underlying function: overfitting.

Ockham's razor, "Entia non sunt multiplicanda praeter necessitatem" (entities must not be multiplied beyond necessity): among the models that explain the training data, prefer the least complex one that still generalizes. In practice: select the model on held-out data, limit the number of units, or regularize (weight decay, early stopping, dropout).

Экзаменатор ждёт противопоставления «represent — learn» и слова noise. Частая ошибка — пересказать теорему и остановиться: вопрос не о ней, а о том, чего она не обещает. У профессора теорема, три «but» и латинская фраза Оккама стоят на одном слайде (лекция 3, слайд 2); первая половина вопроса — это третье «but»: "… but it might be useless in practice if it does not generalize!"

Урок 17 · Бритва Оккама · вопрос 5 из 7 · открыть в уроке

Write the bias–variance decomposition of the expected squared error at a point \(x\). Define each term, state what the expectations are taken over, and say how the terms change with model complexity.

Show model answer

Let \(t = f(x) + \epsilon\), where the noise \(\epsilon\) has zero mean and variance \(\sigma^2\) and is independent of the training set. Let \(g_D\) be the model trained on training set \(D\), and \(\bar g(x) = \E_D[g_D(x)]\) its average prediction. Then

\[ \begin{gathered} \E\bigl[(t - g_D(x))^2\bigr] = \bigl(\bar g(x) - f(x)\bigr)^2 \\ {} + \E_D\bigl[(g_D(x) - \bar g(x))^2\bigr] + \sigma^2 . \end{gathered} \]

The expectations are taken over training sets \(D\) (and over the noise of the new target).

Bias²: systematic error of the average model; high for models that are too simple. Variance: sensitivity of the prediction to the particular training set; high for models that are too flexible. Noise \(\sigma^2\): irreducible, independent of the model.

Increasing complexity lowers the bias and raises the variance, so the expected error is U-shaped; the best complexity minimizes bias² + variance. More training data reduces the variance only.

Полный балл — за квадрат у bias, за третье слагаемое и за фразу «over training sets». Если на экзамене просят вывод, хватит двух шагов из степпера: отделить шум и добавить-вычесть \(\bar g\).

Why is the training error not a good indicator of the performance of a model on future data? How can an independent test set be obtained?

Show model answer

Training error (loss) is not a good indicator of performance on future data for three reasons. (1) The model has been learned from the very same training data, so any estimate based on those data is optimistic. (2) New data will probably not be exactly the same as the training data. (3) Patterns can be found even in random data: a flexible model can fit pure noise and still reach a low training error.

Hence the model must be tested on an independent new test set. There are three ways to get one: someone provides a new dataset; the available data are split and some of them are hidden for later evaluation (hold-out); or the dataset is randomly subsampled with replacement, which is done when the dataset is small. In classification the class distribution must be preserved in every subset, i.e. stratified sampling.

(лекция 3, слайд 4) почти слово в слово: три причины, вывод "We need to test on an independent new test set", три способа и строка про stratified sampling. Хороший пример к третьей причине — сети из загадки, которые подогнались под случайные метки.

Define training dataset, training set, validation set and test set. Then explain why the validation error of the selected model is not a valid final estimate of its generalization error.

Show model answer

The training dataset is the available data: all the labelled samples we have. It is split into disjoint parts. The training set is the data used to learn the model parameters (weights and biases): parameter fitting. The validation set is the data used to perform model selection: choosing hyperparameters, comparing architectures and deciding when to stop training, without entering the gradient. The test set is the data used to perform the final model assessment: it is used only once, at the very end, to estimate the generalization error of the final model.

The validation error of the selected model is optimistically biased: the model was selected precisely because its validation error was the lowest among several noisy estimates, and the minimum of noisy estimates is biased downward. An unbiased estimate requires data that influenced no decision at all, i.e. the test set.

Is \(E_{\text{train}} \le E_{\text{test}}\) always true? Justify your answer.

Show model answer

No. It holds on average for a model obtained by minimising the training error. If \(\hat{\vb{w}}\) minimises \(E_{\text{train}}\) on the training set \(D\) and \(\vb{w}^{*}\) minimises the generalization error, then

\[ \begin{aligned} \E_D\bigl[E_{\text{train}}(\hat{\vb{w}})\bigr] &\le E_{\text{gen}}(\vb{w}^{*}) \\ &\le \E_D\bigl[E_{\text{gen}}(\hat{\vb{w}})\bigr] \end{aligned} \]

In a single run it can fail. The test set is finite, so its error is itself a noisy estimate and can be lower by chance, especially for a small test set. Moreover, with regularisers that act only at training time (dropout, data augmentation) the training error is often higher than the validation or test error.

Слово «always» в вопросе — приманка. Полный балл: «нет», затем «верно в среднем» с причиной (веса подобраны под эти примеры) и хотя бы один контрпример. The available data are shuffled and split once into disjoint training, validation and test sets (e.g. 70/15/15). In classification the split is stratified, i.e. it preserves the class distribution.

The estimate has (i) a pessimistic bias, because the model is trained on only a fraction of the data, and (ii) a variance, because it is computed on a finite held-out set and depends on which samples fell into it: in the words of the slides, the hold-out error "might be biased by the specific split". For an error rate \(p\) measured on \(n\) samples the standard error is about \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\). Holding out more samples reduces the variance but increases the bias, and vice versa.

Hold-out is appropriate when lots of data are available (thousands of samples or more) and training is expensive, so that one training per configuration is all one can afford: the usual situation in deep learning. With few data, cross-validation (leave-one-out or K-fold) is preferable.

Define early stopping. What is \(k_{ES}\), what is the patience, which weights are returned at the end, and why is early stopping considered a regulariser although the error function is not modified?

Show model answer

Some data are held out and the network is trained on the training set only. While training, the error on the hold-out (validation) set, \(E_{\mathrm{val}}(k)\), is computed after every epoch \(k\) together with the training error. The training error keeps decreasing (on average with SGD); the validation error decreases and then increases, when the network starts fitting the noise of the training set and loses generalization. Training is stopped when the validation error increases, and early stopping returns the weights of the epoch

\[ k_{ES} = \argmin_{k}\; E_{\mathrm{val}}(k) , \]

where the validation error takes its lowest value, \(E_{ES} = E_{\mathrm{val}}(k_{ES})\).

Because the validation curve is noisy, training is not stopped at the first increase: it is stopped when \(E_{\mathrm{val}}\) has not improved on its best value for \(P\) consecutive epochs (the patience). A checkpoint of the weights is saved at every new best value and restored at the end, so the model returned is the best one, not the last one.

It is a regulariser because limiting the number of gradient descent steps limits how far the weights can move from their small initial values: the effective capacity is reduced although no weight is removed. For a quadratic error it is approximately equivalent to weight decay with a coefficient \(\gamma\) proportional to \(1/(\eta\tau)\): the fewer the steps, the stronger the regularisation. It requires a validation set, never the test set.

Compare grid search, random search and Bayesian optimisation for hyperparameter tuning. Why is random search usually preferred to grid search when the budget of trainings is the same?

Show model answer

All three minimise the validation error \(E_{\mathrm{val}}(\theta)\) with the same loop (propose a configuration, train, evaluate); they differ in how the next configuration is proposed.

Grid search evaluates every combination of predefined values: simple and parallelisable, but the cost \(\prod_j n_j\) is exponential in the number of hyperparameters. Random search samples \(N\) configurations from chosen distributions (log-uniform for \(\eta\) and \(\gamma\)): the budget \(N\) is chosen freely and the trials run in parallel, but it ignores previous trials and may miss the good region by chance. Bayesian optimisation fits a probabilistic surrogate model to the past trials and chooses the next configuration by maximising an acquisition function that balances exploration (uncertain regions) and exploitation (promising regions): far fewer trainings, but more machinery and inherently sequential.

Random search is preferred to grid search because the validation error usually depends strongly on only a few hyperparameters. An \(n \times n\) grid tests only \(n\) distinct values of the important one, while \(N = n^2\) random trials test \(N\) distinct values of every hyperparameter.

What is regularization? Write the maximum likelihood and the maximum a-posteriori estimates of the weights of a neural network and explain how the second one limits overfitting. Which a-priori distribution of the weights leads to weight decay, and why is it chosen?

Show model answer

Regularization constrains the freedom of the model, on the basis of an a-priori assumption on the model, in order to reduce overfitting.

Maximum likelihood chooses the weights that make the observed data most probable; nothing is assumed about the weights themselves:

\[ \vb{w}_{\mathrm{MLE}} = \argmax_{\vb{w}}\; P(D|\vb{w}) \]

The Bayesian approach reduces the freedom of the model by making an assumption on the a-priori distribution of the parameters, \(P(\vb{w})\), and by maximising the posterior instead of the likelihood (Bayes' rule; \(P(D)\) does not depend on \(\vb{w}\)):

\[ \begin{aligned} \vb{w}_{\mathrm{MAP}} &= \argmax_{\vb{w}}\; P(\vb{w}|D) \\ &= \argmax_{\vb{w}}\; P(D|\vb{w})\,P(\vb{w}) \end{aligned} \]

Weights that are improbable under the prior are selected only if the data support them strongly, so the network is no longer free to fit the noise. Weight decay assumes that the weights are small: every weight is independently distributed as \(w_q \sim N(0, \sigma_w^2)\). The motivation is empirical: neural networks with small weights are observed to generalize better.

Определение без слов про априорное предположение: «регуляризация борется с переобучением» называет цель, а не способ. И prior без нулевого среднего: именно ноль в \(N(0, \sigma_w^2)\) означает «веса малы».

Describe dropout at training time and at test time. Why is a scaling factor needed? State where the factor is applied in (a) the weight scaling rule and (b) inverted dropout.

Training. Let \(p\) be the probability of keeping a unit. At every iteration each hidden unit is kept with probability \(p\) and set to zero with probability \(1 - p\), independently of the other units: \(\tilde h_j = m_j h_j\) with \(m_j \sim Be(p)\). A new mask is sampled every time, so different mini-batches train different thinned networks; the same mask is used in the forward and in the backward pass, so only the weights of the sampled thinned network are updated.

Test. The masks are removed: no unit is dropped. Since \(\E[\tilde h_j] = p\,h_j\), the next layer was trained on inputs that are on average \(p\) times the full ones; with all units active, the weighted sums it receives would be \(1/p\) times larger than their average during training, so its units would work in a regime they were not trained for.

(a) Weight scaling rule: use the full network and multiply the activations of the dropout layer (equivalently, its outgoing weights) by \(p\) at test time; this single pass approximately averages the outputs of all the thinned networks. (b) Inverted dropout: divide the kept activations by \(p\) during training, \(\tilde h_j = m_j h_j / p\), and use the plain network at test time. Both make the expected pre-activation of the next layer the same in training and at test time.

Give two reasons why dropout reduces overfitting. How many thinned networks does a hidden layer with 10 units define? How does dropout differ from bagging?

Show model answer

1. It prevents co-adaptation. By randomly turning off some neurons we force each hidden unit to learn an independent feature: it cannot rely on specific other units being present, so its feature has to be useful together with many random subsets of the other units. The representation becomes redundant and robust.

2. It behaves as an ensemble method. A layer with 10 droppable units defines \(2^{10} = 1024\) thinned networks that share weights. Dropout trains these weaker classifiers on different mini-batches: each training iteration updates one randomly sampled thinned network. At test time the masks are removed and we implicitly average the responses of all ensemble members: weight scaling approximates the average of their predictions, which reduces variance.

Bagging trains a few independent models, each with its own weights, to convergence on bootstrap samples and averages their outputs explicitly. Dropout trains exponentially many weight-sharing sub-networks, each for at most a few steps, and averages them implicitly with a single forward pass.

What is the vanishing gradient problem? Which layers does it affect most, and why do sigmoid and tanh activations cause it? Name three remedies.

Show model answer

In backpropagation the error signal of a hidden unit is built from the signals of the next layer:

\[ \delta_i^{(l-1)} = g'\big(a_i^{(l-1)}\big) \sum_j w_{ji}^{(l)}\, \delta_j^{(l)} \]

Unrolling this recursion, the gradient that reaches the first layer is a product of \(L-1\) per-layer factors of size about \(\abs{w \cdot g'(a)}\). For the sigmoid \(g' \le 0.25\) (and \(g' \approx 0\) when the unit is saturated); for tanh \(g' \le 1\) (and \(\approx 0\) when saturated). With factors below 1 the product shrinks exponentially with depth, so the layers closest to the input receive almost no gradient and their weights are not updated, although the error is still large.

The slides show it on the gradient of a first-layer weight in a network with one hidden layer, \(\partial E / \partial w_{ji}^{(1)}\): it already contains two activation derivatives, \(g_1'\) and \(h_j'\). Sigmoid and tanh saturate, so this derivative gets close to zero and is never above 1; backpropagation multiplies such derivatives, hence the gradient far away from the output vanishes and learning in a deep network does not happen. The problem is best known from recurrent neural networks, but it affects every deep network.

Remedies: non-saturating activations such as ReLU (\(g' = 1\) for \(a \gt 0\)); variance-preserving weight initialisation (Xavier, He); batch normalisation; residual connections.

Проверяют три вещи: механизм (произведение множителей по слоям, а не одно слово saturation), кто страдает (слои у входа) и конкретные лекарства. Сильный ответ отдельно говорит, что у sigmoid затухание есть и без насыщения, потому что \(g' \le 0.25\). Define the ReLU activation function and its derivative. List its advantages and disadvantages. What is a dying ReLU, and how do Leaky ReLU and ELU address it?

Show model answer

\(g(a) = \max(0, a)\); \(g'(a) = 1_{a \gt 0}\), i.e. 1 for \(a \gt 0\) and 0 for \(a \lt 0\) (not differentiable at 0; by convention \(g'(0) = 0\)).

Advantages: faster SGD convergence (about 6 times faster than tanh in Krizhevsky et al., 2012); sparse activation (only part of the hidden units are active); efficient gradient propagation: it does not saturate for \(a \gt 0\), so the backward factor \(g'\) of an active unit is exactly 1 and the activation itself neither shrinks nor amplifies the gradient (the weights still can); efficient computation (just a threshold at zero, no exponential). Not on the slides, but true: it is scale-invariant, \(\max(0, c\,a) = c \max(0, a)\) for \(c \ge 0\).

Disadvantages: not differentiable at zero (but differentiable anywhere else); output not zero-centred; unbounded output, which could potentially blow up; dying neurons.

A ReLU neuron dies when it is pushed into a state in which it is inactive for essentially all inputs: its pre-activation is negative for all training samples. It happens with high learning rates (one too large update), and also with a large negative bias or after a poor initialisation. Its output is 0 and \(g' = 0\), so no gradient flows backward through the neuron: its \(\delta\) and the gradients of its weights and bias are exactly zero, it is stuck and is never updated again. Many dead neurons mean a decreased model capacity. Leaky ReLU fixes the dying ReLU problem with a small slope on the left: \(g(a) = 0.01\,a\) for \(a \lt 0\) (in general \(\alpha a\)), so the gradient there is \(\alpha\) instead of 0. ELU uses \(g(a) = \alpha(e^{a} - 1)\) for \(a \lt 0\), with gradient \(\alpha e^{a} \gt 0\); it also makes the mean activation closer to zero, which speeds up learning, and its \(\alpha\) is tuned by hand.

Derive the Xavier initialization as seen in class. Start from a linear neuron with \(I\) inputs and random weights, state your assumptions and obtain the condition that keeps the variance of the output equal to the variance of the input. Then give the analogous condition for the gradient and the final formula of Glorot & Bengio.

Show model answer

Consider a linear neuron with \(I\) inputs \(x_1, \dots, x_I\) and random weights; the bias is initialized to zero. Its output is

\[ h_j = w_{j1}x_1 + \dots + w_{jI}x_I \]

The weights are drawn independently of the inputs, so the variance of one term is

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{Var}(w_{ji}x_i) &= \E[x_i]^2\,\mathrm{Var}(w_{ji}) \\ &\quad {} + \E[w_{ji}]^2\,\mathrm{Var}(x_i) \\ &\quad {} + \mathrm{Var}(w_{ji})\,\mathrm{Var}(x_i) \end{aligned} \]

With zero-mean inputs and zero-mean weights the first two terms vanish:

\[ \mathrm{Var}(w_{ji}x_i) = \mathrm{Var}(w_{ji})\,\mathrm{Var}(x_i) \]

Assuming that the weights and the inputs are i.i.d., the \(I\) terms are independent and have the same variance, so

\[ \mathrm{Var}(h_j) = I \cdot \mathrm{Var}(w_i)\,\mathrm{Var}(x_i) \]

The output variance is the input variance scaled by \(I \cdot \mathrm{Var}(w_i)\). The output of one layer is the input of the next, so the variance stays the same across layers only if

\[ I \cdot \mathrm{Var}(w) = 1 \]

that is \(w \sim N(0,\ 1/n_{in})\) with \(n_{in} = I\); the second argument is the variance. The same reasoning for the gradient, which a linear neuron collects from the \(n_{out}\) units it feeds,

\[ \delta_i = \sum_{j=1}^{n_{out}} w_{ji}\,\delta_j \]

gives \(n_{out}\,\mathrm{Var}(w) = 1\). Both conditions hold only if \(n_{in} = n_{out}\), so Glorot & Bengio propose the compromise

\[ w \sim N\Bigl(0,\ \frac{2}{n_{in} + n_{out}}\Bigr) \]

The linear neuron is a strong assumption, but tanh is close to linear around zero and in practice the formula works.

Why can the weights of a Feed Forward Neural Network not all be initialized to zero, or to the same constant?

Show model answer

With identical weights all neurons of a hidden layer compute the same output and receive the same gradient, so after every update they are still identical: the symmetry is never broken and the layer is equivalent to a single neuron.

With all-zero weights it is even worse. With tanh or ReLU hidden units the hidden outputs are \(g(0) = 0\), so the gradients of the next layer's weights, \(\delta_j h_i\), are zero, and the hidden \(\delta\) are zero because they are multiplied by zero weights. All the gradients of the weights are zero and no learning happens: only the output bias can move, so the network learns at most a constant. With sigmoid hidden units \(g(0) = 0.5\), so the gradients are not all zero, but they are identical within a layer, which is again the symmetry problem.

Random initialization breaks the symmetry; its scale must then be chosen so that the variance of activations and gradients is preserved across layers (Xavier, He). Biases can be initialized to zero.

Write the Batch Normalization transform applied to the pre-activations \(x_1, \dots, x_m\) of one unit over a mini-batch (a) at training time and (b) at test time. Explain why the learnable parameters \(\gamma\) and \(\beta\) are introduced.

(a) Training: mean and stddev are computed for each mini-batch, separately for each unit; the unit's pre-activation is normalized (mean subtraction, stddev division), then scaled and shifted.

\[ \mu_B = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} x_i \] \[ \sigma_B^2 = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(x_i - \mu_B)^2 \] \[ \hat x_i = \frac{x_i - \mu_B}{\sqrt{\sigma_B^2 + \epsilon}} \] \[ y_i = \gamma\,\hat x_i + \beta \equiv \mathrm{BN}_{\gamma,\beta}(x_i) \]

Backpropagation takes the normalization into account. Meanwhile running averages are updated: \(\mu_{\mathrm{run}} \leftarrow (1-\alpha)\mu_{\mathrm{run}} + \alpha\mu_B\), and the same for \(\sigma^2_{\mathrm{run}}\).

(b) Test: the global mean / stddev are used instead of the mini-batch ones; these global statistics are the running averages estimated during training. They are frozen, so the output is a deterministic function of the single input and does not depend on the other samples.

\[ y = \gamma\,\frac{x - \mu_{\mathrm{run}}}{\sqrt{\sigma^2_{\mathrm{run}} + \epsilon}} + \beta \]

\(\gamma\) and \(\beta\): normalisation alone would force every unit to zero mean and unit variance and would limit the representational power of the layer. The scale and shift step is a linear transformation that lets the network decide (learn) how much normalization it needs: it can give each unit any mean and scale, and even undo the normalisation with \(\gamma = \sigma\), \(\beta = \mu\). These become two directly learnable quantities, decoupled from the weights of the preceding layers.

What is internal covariate shift, and how does Batch Normalization cope with it? State where the Batch Normalization layer is placed in the network.

Show model answer

Networks converge faster if their inputs have been whitened (zero mean, unit variances) and are uncorrelated. The same issue appears inside the network: during training the distribution of the inputs of each hidden layer keeps changing, because the weights of the preceding layers are updated. This is internal covariate shift, so normalization is useful also at the level of hidden layers.

Batch Normalization normalizes each unit's pre-activation with the mean and stddev of the current mini-batch. This forces the activations to take values on a unit Gaussian (zero mean, unit variance) at the beginning of the training; a learnable scale \(\gamma\) and shift \(\beta\) then let the network decide how much normalization it needs. The BatchNorm layer is added after fully connected (or convolutional) layers and before the nonlinearities: Fully Connected → Batch Norm → ReLU. It can be interpreted as doing preprocessing at every layer of the network, but integrated into the network itself in a differentiable way.

List four effects that Batch Normalization has been shown to have on the training of a network, and give a one-sentence reason for each.

Show model answer

1. It improves the gradient flow through the network: the pre-activations stay in the non-saturating range of the nonlinearity, so the gradients do not vanish.

4. It acts as a form of regularization, slightly reducing the need for dropout: at training time the output for a sample depends on the other samples of its mini-batch, which adds noise.

Compare batch gradient descent, stochastic gradient descent (SGD) and mini-batch gradient descent. For each of them write the gradient used for one weight update, and explain the trade-off.

Show model answer

Batch gradient descent uses all \(N\) samples:

\[ \pd{E(w)}{w} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} \pd{E(x_n, w)}{w} \]

SGD uses a single sample:

\[ \pd{E(w)}{w} \approx \pd{E(x_n, w)}{w} \]

Mini-batch gradient descent uses a subset of \(M \lt N\) samples:

\[ \pd{E(w)}{w} \approx \frac{1}{M}\sum_{n \in \text{mini-batch}} \pd{E(x_n, w)}{w} \]

Batch: the gradient is exact, but every update costs a pass over the whole dataset (one update per epoch), which is impractical for large \(N\). SGD: \(N\) cheap updates per epoch; the estimate is unbiased but has high variance, so the trajectory is noisy. Mini-batch: still unbiased, with variance reduced by a factor \(M\) (noise \(\propto 1/\sqrt{M}\)); it is a good trade-off between variance and computation and exploits GPU parallelism. Because of the \(1/\sqrt{M}\) law, larger batches give diminishing returns.

Проверяют три вещи: три формулы, слова unbiased / high variance и то, что mini-batch — компромисс между дисперсией и вычислениями. Закон \(1/\sqrt{M}\) — то, что отличает отличный ответ от хорошего.

Write the gradient-descent update rule with momentum. Explain why momentum helps when the error surface has a narrow valley (a ravine).

Show model answer

As seen in class:

\[ \begin{aligned} w^{k+1} = w^{k} &- \eta\,\pd{E(w)}{w}\Big|_{w^{k}} \\ &- \alpha\,\pd{E(w)}{w}\Big|_{w^{k-1}} \end{aligned} \]

The standard (velocity) form of the same idea accumulates all past gradients, not only the previous one:

\[ \begin{aligned} v_k &= \alpha\,v_{k-1} - \eta\,\nabla E(w_k) \\ w_{k+1} &= w_k + v_k \end{aligned} \]

with \(0 \le \alpha \lt 1\), typically 0.9. In a ravine a single learning rate is limited by the steep direction, so plain gradient descent either zig-zags across the valley or crawls along its flat bottom. The velocity is an exponentially weighted sum of past gradients: components that keep the same sign (along the valley) add up, up to a factor \(1/(1-\alpha)\); components that change sign (across the valley) cancel. The same inertia helps to roll through small local minima and plateaus.

Write the Nesterov accelerated gradient update as seen in class and explain how it differs from momentum.

Show model answer

Nesterov accelerated gradient: make a jump as momentum, then adjust.

\[ w^{k+\frac{1}{2}} = w^{k} - \alpha\,\pd{E(w)}{w}\Big|_{w^{k-1}} \] \[ w^{k+1} = w^{k} - \eta\,\pd{E(w)}{w}\Big|_{w^{k+\frac{1}{2}}} \]

The first line is the momentum jump to an intermediate (look-ahead) point \(w^{k+\frac{1}{2}}\). The second line is the correction: a gradient step whose gradient is evaluated at the look-ahead point instead of \(w^k\). The correction is applied after the jump, so the whole update is

\[ \begin{aligned} w^{k+1} = w^{k} &- \alpha\,\pd{E(w)}{w}\Big|_{w^{k-1}} \\ &- \eta\,\pd{E(w)}{w}\Big|_{w^{k+\frac{1}{2}}} \end{aligned} \]

Momentum has the same two terms, but both gradients are computed at points already visited, \(w^k\) and \(w^{k-1}\): it measures the gradient first and then jumps. Nesterov jumps first and measures the gradient where the jump lands. If the jump overshoots, that gradient already points back, so the step is corrected immediately and not one iteration later: fewer oscillations, less overshooting. In the standard notation with a velocity:

\[ \begin{aligned} v_k &= \alpha\,v_{k-1} - \eta\,\nabla E(w_k + \alpha\,v_{k-1}) \\ w_{k+1} &= w_k + v_k \end{aligned} \]

Ждут две строки слайда 39 лекции 3 и мысль the gradient is evaluated after the jump. Вторую строку слайд начинает с \(w^k\): пиши как на слайде, а следующей фразой скажи, что поправка делается из точки после прыжка. Запись через скорость — запасной вариант, если формула слайда не вспомнится.

Урок 27 · Ложка супа и тяжёлый шар · вопрос 8 из 8 · открыть в уроке

Why are adaptive learning rates useful when training a deep network? Name three algorithms that implement them and say in one sentence what each of them does.

Show model answer

Neurons in each layer learn differently: gradient magnitudes vary across layers, and early layers get "vanishing gradients". A single learning rate is therefore too large for some weights and too small for others; ideally every weight should have its own learning rate, adapted during training.

Algorithms (any three):

  • Rprop (Riedmiller and Braun): uses only the sign of the gradient and keeps a separate step size for every weight, increased while the sign stays the same and decreased when it flips; it needs full-batch gradients.
  • AdaGrad (Duchi et al.): divides the learning rate of every weight by the square root of the sum of all its past squared gradients, so the step can only shrink.
  • RMSprop (Tieleman and Hinton): "SGD + Rprop" — divides the gradient by the square root of a running average of its squared values, so it works with mini-batches and the step does not shrink to zero.
  • AdaDelta (Zeiler): like RMSprop, and it also replaces the global learning rate with the root of a running average of the squared past updates, so no learning rate has to be set.
  • Adam (Kingma and Ba): RMSprop plus momentum — running averages of the gradient and of its square, with bias correction.
