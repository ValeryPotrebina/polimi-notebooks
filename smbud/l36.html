Holes, caps and spikes: missing values, censoring and outliers

After this lesson you will find the holes that hide in a data set, decide whether to drop or fill them, tell censored from truncated data, and explain why a z-score can miss an outlier that your eye sees at once.

The slide on p. 1120 prints eleven numbers, 3, 4, 7, 4, 8, 3, 9, 5, 7, 6, 92, and calls the 92 "suspicious". You saw it before you finished reading. Now test it the way Applied Statistics taught you: compute its z-score, its distance from the mean in standard deviations, and flag it if the score is above 3. The 92 scores 3.006: flagged, but only by a hair.

Make it worse. Replace the 92 by 920, or by 92 million. Write down your guess for the new z-score: 30? 300?

Both times it is 3.015, and no number in a list of eleven can ever score more. Section 5 shows why, and which yardstick puts the 92 at a distance of 29. On the way there, the lesson meets the other ways a data set lies by omission: holes, capped values and spikes.

Munging, and why a number needs a meaning

Lesson 35 followed the wrangling steps of the slides. Before the statistics, the deck adds one more word (p. 1102):

Data munging — "Potentially lossy transformations applied to a piece of data or a file"; "Vague data transformation steps that are not yet completely clear"; "E.g., removing punctuation or html tags, data parsing, filtering, and transformation." (p. 1102)

Both properties are warnings. Lossy: strip the punctuation and "U.S." and "US" become one string, with no way back; strip the HTML tags and the table that said which number was the price is gone. Vague: you munge while still finding out what the data needs, so nobody writes the steps down. Hence the habit: keep the raw file, munge a copy, record each step as a replayable script (the tools are in lesson 37).

Holes you can see, and holes that hide

The deck looks for missing data in two layers (pp. 1105–1106): holes you can see if you look, and damage that leaves the file looking complete.

  • "Match data specifications against data - are all the attributes present?"
  • "Scan individual records - are there gaps?"
  • "Rough checks : number of files, file sizes, number of records, number of duplicates"
  • "Compare estimates (averages, frequencies, medians) with “expected” values and bounds; check at various levels of granularity since aggregates can be misleading"
  • "Values are truncated or censored - check for spikes and dips in distributions and histograms"
  • "Missing values and defaults are indistinguishable - too many missing values? metadata or domain expertise can help"
  • "Errors of omission e.g. all calls from a particular area are missing - check if data are missing randomly or are localized in some way"

Mind the last overt check: aggregates can be misleading. A monthly total of calls can be right while one week is missing and another was loaded twice; the errors cancel in the total and show only per week, region or source.

Hidden damage can pass every count. If a loader writes 0 for an absent value, a customer who made no calls and one whose minutes were lost look the same, and only metadata or domain knowledge can tell them apart. The cure is upstream: store a real empty value, never a default that looks like data.

Three ways a value goes missing

"Missing Values: Random" (p. 1107) is a bar chart of visitors per day: days 11, 12 and 16 have no bar, and beside it stand "System failures" and "Complete miss".

A failed collector removes whole records, and nothing about those days caused it: the holes fall at random. Leave them as holes. A bar of 0 would claim that nobody visited; an empty slot says that nobody knows. The mean per recorded day stays fair, while the monthly total is three days short.

"Missing Values: Wrong Ingestion" (p. 1108) is a "CSV to table / excel" accident. Three raw lines go into a six-column table:

Antony Mc James IV, 123 Untidy Cir, Suite# 234, Cumbersome, TX, 76849
Miller Johnson, 1102 Messy Data St, Middletow, OH
Betty Flyier 6483 Phew Lane, Apt A4, FixMe, CA, 91103

The loader splits at the commas and fills Customer, Address Line 1, Address Line 2, City, State and Zip from left to right. Line 1 has six values and lands correctly. Line 2 has four; line 3 has five, because the comma after the name is missing. The slide calls the defects "Missing fields" and "Merged fields" (Fig. 3).

Every value was in the file: the loader created the holes and put wrong values beside them ("OH" is now a city). A check that only counts empty cells may pass the file. The fix belongs in the parser — quote the fields, compare each line's number of values with the header — not in a statistical estimate.

Row 3 of the loaded table, "Betty Flyier 6483 Phew Lane | Apt A4 | FixMe | CA | 91103 |", is shaded green like the correct row 1; only row 2, "Miller Johnson | 1102 Messy Data St | Middletow | OH | |", is orange.

Row 3 is broken too, and worse than row 2: it is the slide's own "Merged fields" case. Only row 1 is loaded correctly.

Its raw line has five values for six columns: the name swallowed the street and every later value moved one column left. The exam may be matched against the slide as printed: know its two labels and which row shows which — "Missing fields" is row 2, "Merged fields" is row 3, whatever the shading says.

"Missing Values: Inapplicability" (p. 1109) circles two blocks of a medical form, the women's health history and the men's (a PSA blood test for the prostate); every patient leaves one of them empty. The slide's two lines: "Partial data by nature", "Remember to leave empty slots".

Here an empty cell is a statement, not a hole. Filling it invents a PSA test for Anna; a default such as 1900-01-01 recreates the value-or-default confusion; dropping incomplete records deletes every patient. The schema must let the slot stay empty.

Three kinds of holes, three cures: estimate the random ones, fix the ingestion ones in the loader, leave the inapplicable ones empty.

Drop the record, or fill the hole?

Why not simply ignore every record with a hole? The slide "Imputing Values to Missing Data" (p. 1110) answers:

  • "In federated data, between 30%-70% of the data points will have at least one missing attribute - data wastage if we ignore all records with a missing value"
  • "Remaining data is seriously biased"
  • "Lack of confidence in results"
  • "Understanding pattern of missing data unearths data integrity issues"

Federated data comes from several sources, each contributing some of the attributes. Why do so many records have a hole when each attribute is missing only now and then? Because the chances multiply:

  1. A record has \(p\) attributes. Each is missing with probability \(q\), so each is present with probability \(1 - q\).

  2. The record is complete only if every attribute is present. If the holes fall independently, the probabilities multiply:

    \[ P(\text{complete}) = (1 - q)^p \]

  3. Ten attributes with 5 % holes each:

    \[ 0.95^{10} = 0.599 \]

    40 % of the records have a hole although 95 % of every column is there. With twenty attributes, \(0.95^{20} = 0.36\): 64 % are lost. Both land inside the slide's 30–70 %.

Wastage is the visible cost; bias is the hidden one. The survivors are a fair sample only if the holes fall purely at random. If heavy callers come from a sloppier source and lose fields more often, the complete records are mostly light callers, and their average bill is too low. The widget shows both, and the two simplest fills.

The last line of p. 1110 turns the problem around: the pattern of the holes is itself a finding. Holes from one source only mean a broken feed, holes on one date a failed load; and the pattern tells you what not to fill at all: ingestion holes and inapplicable slots.

The imputation ladder

To impute is to fill a hole with an estimate. The deck climbs a ladder; each rung uses more of the data and costs more.

Rung 1 is "Standalone imputation" (p. 1111): fill the hole with "Mean, median, other point estimates" of its own column. The slide adds: "Assume: Distribution of the missing values is the same as the non-missing values."; "Does not take into account inter-relationships"; "Introduces bias"; "Convenient, easy to implement". The widget showed the bias: filled values sit at the centre, so the spread shrinks and correlations weaken.

Rung 2, "Better imputation - use attribute relationships" (p. 1112), needs a condition on the pattern of the holes: "Assume : all prior attributes are populated", "That is, monotonicity in missing values." The slide's table is its picture (Fig. 4).

Monotone missing pattern — the attributes can be ordered \(X_1, X_2, \dots, X_p\) so that whenever \(X_j\) is missing, every attribute after it is missing too.

Monotonicity lets you fill the table column by column, left to right, always predicting from columns already full. The slide names "Two techniques": "Regression (parametric)" and "Propensity score (nonparametric)". The first is the "Regression method" (p. 1113): "Use linear regression, sweep left-to-right",

\[ X_3 = a + b X_2 + c X_1 \] \[ X_4 = d + e X_3 + f X_2 + g X_1 \]

"and so on", where "X3 in the second equation is estimated from the first equation if it is missing". The slide gives no numbers, so fit the first equation on its own table. It is a toy: three complete rows for three unknowns.

  1. Rows 1–3 have \(X_3\); row 4 does not. Write the first equation for the three complete rows:

    \[ 3.5 = a + 20b + 1.0c \] \[ 4.0 = a + 18b + 1.1c \] \[ 2.2 = a + 22b + 1.9c \]

  2. Subtract the first line from the other two, and \(a\) cancels:

    \[ 0.5 = -2b + 0.1c \] \[ -1.3 = 2b + 0.9c \]

  3. Add these two, and \(b\) cancels: \(c = -0.8\). Back-substitute: \(b = -0.29\), then \(a = 3.5 + 5.8 + 0.8 = 10.1\).

  4. Fill the hole of row 4, where \(X_1 = 0.9\) and \(X_2 = 15\):

    \[ \hat X_3 = 10.1 - 0.29 \cdot 15 - 0.8 \cdot 0.9 \] \[ \hat X_3 = 5.03 \]

  5. Compare with rung 1: the mean of the observed \(X_3\) is \(9.7 / 3 = 3.23\), the median 3.5. Three methods, three answers; and 5.03 is an extrapolation, since \(X_2 = 15\) lies below the observed 18 to 22.

The second technique, "Propensity Scores (nonparametric)" (p. 1114), models the chance that \(X_j\) is missing instead of its value:

  1. "Let Yj=1 if Xj is missing, 0 otherwise"
  2. "Estimate P(Yj =1) based on X1 through X(j-1) using logistic regression"
  3. "Group by propensity score P(Yj =1)"
  4. "Within each group, estimate missing Xjs from known Xjs using approximate Bayesian bootstrap."
  5. "Repeat until all attributes are populated."
Logistic regression is the Applied Statistics model for a yes/no outcome; here the outcome is "is the value missing?".

Records with about the same score, the same chance of a hole, are treated as comparable, and each hole gets a value resampled from the observed values of its group (the approximate Bayesian bootstrap). Hence "nonparametric": no formula for \(X_j\) is assumed, although the score comes from a parametric logistic regression.

Rung 3 handles an "Arbitrary missing pattern" with "Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)" (p. 1115). It assumes that the "data is multivariate Normal, with parameter Θ" (means and covariances) and alternates "(1) Simulate missing X, given Θ estimated from observed X ; (2) Re-compute Θ using filled in X", "Repeat until stable." It is "Expensive: Used most often to induce monotonicity": MCMC fills just enough cells to make a staircase, and the cheap sweep does the rest. The slide ends, in bold:

"Note that imputed values are useful in aggregates but can’t be trusted individually"

The widget measured it (task 4): regression filling missed the mean bill by 0.10 €, a single filled bill by 6.33 € on average. Errors cancel in an average; one guess stays a guess.

Caps and cuts: censored and truncated data

Some damage is done by a bound. The slide "Censoring and Truncation" (p. 1116) calls it "Well studied in Biostatistics, relevant to time dependent data e.g. duration" and separates two cases.

Censored — "Measurement is bounded but not precise e.g. Call duration > 20 are recorded as 20". The record stays; its value is replaced by the bound.

Truncated — "Data point dropped if it exceeds or falls below a certain bound e.g. customers with less than 2 minutes of calling per month". The record is gone.

A censored call of 47 minutes is in the table as 20: you know only "at least 20". A truncated customer who called for one minute is not in the table at all, and nothing in it says that anyone is missing. Fig. 5 shows why p. 1106 says "check for spikes and dips".

The two bias the mean in opposite directions (background): capping long calls lowers the average duration, dropping light users makes the average customer heavier. Mnemonic: censored is capped, truncated is thrown out.

Background, not on the slides. In the Second World War the military wanted to armour the parts of returning bombers that showed the most bullet holes. Abraham Wald pointed out that only survivors were in the data: the armour belonged where the survivors showed no damage, because planes hit there had not come back. The lost planes were truncated out of the data.

Intervals the study only half sees

Durations are where censoring is at home. The figure "Censored time intervals" (p. 1117) draws a study window between "Begin Study" and "End Study" and six intervals against it (Fig. 6).

The course notes (p. 86) describe this picture as values that "will be truncated". Keep the slides' word: the intervals are in the data, so they are censored.

An interval that began before the window is left censored: its start is unknown. One still running at the end is right censored: you know only that it lasted at least this long. Only "Complete" has a precise duration. Intervals 1 and 2 make the point: identical in the data, very different in reality. Read both as ending at End Study and both durations come out too short, the second by far. Biostatistics meets this daily: a patient still alive when a study ends has a right-censored survival time.

The bias can be mitigated on one condition, stated on p. 1118: "If censoring/truncation mechanism not known, analysis can be inaccurate and biased." "But if you know the mechanism, you can mitigate the bias from the analysis." Hence "Metadata should record the existence as well as the nature of censoring/truncation": capped at 20 minutes, say, or customers under 2 minutes excluded.

Spikes

The last picture (p. 1119) is a plot titled "Potential Data Problem": a noisy "Count" over "Current Accounts" with one spike above 600 and a smaller one above 200, and the caption "Spikes usually indicate censored time intervals caused by resetting of timestamps to defaults". When a system loses an account's real start date and writes a default instead (say, the date of a data migration), you know only that the account existed by then — its interval is censored — and every such account piles onto one value: defaults standing in for missing values.

Censored: the record stays and its value is capped. Truncated: the record is dropped. Both can show in a histogram, as a spike or a dip, and their bias can be mitigated only if metadata records them.

Departure from the expected: outliers

Back to the hook. The slide "Suspicious Data" (p. 1120) lists the eleven numbers and says "“92” is suspicious - an outlier". Then it refuses to convict: "Outliers are potentially legitimate"; "Often, they are data or model glitches"; "Or, they could be a data miner’s dream, e.g. highly profitable customers".

Three verdicts, all possible: a real but rare value; a glitch of the data (a typo for 9, a unit error, a default) or of the model (a wrong "expected" makes a normal value look odd); or your most valuable record, the big spender, the fraud worth finding. Deleting outliers automatically throws the third kind out with the second.

"Outliers must be removed before the analysis." The course notes (p. 86) do call outliers records "which need to be eliminated", but the slides say the opposite — potentially legitimate, possibly a data miner's dream — and the slides win. Investigate, then decide.

Outliers also move aggregates. Without the 92 the ten numbers have mean 5.6 and median 5.5; with it, mean 13.45 and median 6. One record in eleven more than doubled the mean; the median barely noticed.

Two yardsticks

The classic test divides the distance from the mean by the standard deviation. The robust test (background, not on the slide) uses ingredients an outlier cannot drag: the median and the median absolute deviation, MAD.

\[ z = \frac{x - \bar x}{s} \] \[ z_{\text{rob}} = \frac{x - \tilde x}{1.4826 \cdot \text{MAD}} \] \[ \text{MAD} = \operatorname{median}_i \abs{x_i - \tilde x} \]
\(\bar x, s\)
mean and sample standard deviation
\(\tilde x\)
median
\(\text{MAD}\)
median of the distances from the median
\(1.4826\)
makes MAD comparable to \(s\) for normally distributed data

For the slide's numbers, \(\bar x = 13.45\) and \(s = 26.13\), so the 92 has \(z = 3.006\). The median is 6, the distances from it are 3, 2, 1, 2, 2, 3, 3, 1, 1, 0 and 86, their median is 2, so \(z_{\text{rob}} = 86 / 2.965 = 29.0\). One yardstick says "barely", the other "very far".

Why the classic score cannot pass 3.015

The masking is built into the formula (background, not on the slide): the outlier sits inside the mean and the standard deviation that judge it. Follow the most extreme case through:

  1. Put \(n - 1\) values equal to \(c\) and one at \(c + d\). The mean moves only a little:

    \[ \bar x = c + \frac{d}{n} \]

  2. The odd value lies \(d\,(n-1)/n\) above the mean, each of the others \(d/n\) below it. Add up the squared deviations:

    \[ (n-1)\,\frac{d^2}{n^2} + \frac{(n-1)^2\, d^2}{n^2} \] \[ = \frac{(n-1)\, d^2}{n} \]

  3. Divide by \(n - 1\) for the sample variance: \(s^2 = d^2 / n\), so \(s = d / \sqrt{n}\). The yardstick grows in proportion to the outlier's own distance.

  4. Divide the distance by the yardstick, and \(d\) cancels:

    \[ z = \frac{d\,(n-1)/n}{d / \sqrt{n}} = \frac{n-1}{\sqrt{n}} \]

    For \(n = 11\), \(10 / \sqrt{11} = 3.015\). Spreading the other values apart only lowers the score, so no value in a sample of eleven can score more.

The puzzle, resolved

The hook asked for the z-score of 920 and of 92 million among the ten small numbers. Both give 3.015, the ceiling \((n-1)/\sqrt{n}\) for eleven values: as the outlier moves away, it stretches the standard deviation as fast as its own distance grows. With eleven values a 3-sigma rule can flag a value only by a hair; among the slide's numbers it misses anything below about 72, and it misses both copies when the 92 comes twice. The robust yardstick does not move with the outlier: the 92 stands 29 robust units from the median, the 920 more than 300. The boxplot rule of Fig. 1 catches it too: for the eleven numbers the upper fence is \(7.5 + 1.5 \cdot 3.5 = 12.75\).

Many ways to define "expected"

The next slide (p. 1121) turns the example into a definition: "Outlier – “departure from the expected”", and "Types of outliers – defining “expected”". Every detection method states what you expected; the slide lists "Many approaches".

Your Applied Statistics labs met the geometric kind already: the noise points of DBSCAN are points that no dense neighbourhood claims.

An outlier is a departure from the expected; every method is a way of defining "expected", and the verdict — legitimate, glitch or a data miner's dream — needs the meaning of the data.

Control charts: the expected band, for one variable and for many

Control charts come from "Quality control of production lots" (p. 1122): every hour a factory measures a small sample of its output and asks whether the process has changed. They are "Typically univariate: X-Bar, R, CUSUM": an X-bar chart plots the mean of each sample, an R chart its range (largest minus smallest value), a CUSUM chart the cumulative sum of the deviations from the target, which catches a small persistent shift. The "Main steps (based on statistical inference)":

  1. "Define “expected” and “departure” e.g. Mean and standard error based on sampling distribution of sample mean (aggregate);"
  2. "Compute aggregate each sample"
  3. "Plot aggregates vs expected and error bounds"
  4. "“Out of Control” if aggregates fall outside bounds"

For data quality a sample is a batch: one day's file, one source, one load (Fig. 7).

The bounds are usually three standard errors (background), \(\bar{\bar x} \pm 3\,\sigma / \sqrt{n}\) for samples of \(n\) items: standard errors, because the plotted point is an aggregate. This also explains "Distributional assumptions for charts not based on means e.g. R–charts": by the central limit theorem sample means are close to normal whatever the data, so an X-bar chart needs few assumptions; ranges enjoy no such theorem, so R-chart limits assume a normal population.

The kilogram example

"An Example" (p. 1123) is a real chart from the NIST handbook, "Shewhart control chart for kilogram calibrations": the "Corrections (mg)" of a reference kilogram calibrated again and again from the mid-1970s to the late 1980s. It prints "UCL = mean + 3s = -19.39 mg" and "LCL = mean - 3s = -19.57 mg", and the rest follows:

\[ \bar x = \frac{-19.39 - 19.57}{2} = -19.48 \ \text{mg} \] \[ s = \frac{-19.39 + 19.57}{6} = 0.03 \ \text{mg} \]

The limits are \(6s\) apart, hence the 6. Each point is a single calibration, not the mean of a sample, so its standard error is \(s\) itself and the bounds are \(\pm 3s\). A few points around 1987 reach the upper limit: those measurements get investigated. Under normality a point crosses a 3s limit by chance 0.27 % of the time, once in about 370 (background).

Two variables: the rectangle and the ellipse

Watch two correlated variables with one chart each and you get the rectangle of Fig. 8: a point is "in control" when it is inside both pairs of limits. "Multivariate Control Charts - 1" (p. 1124) says why that is not enough: bivariate charts are "based on bivariate Normal assumptions", and "component-wise limits lead to Type I, II errors", because the region of expected points is an ellipse tilted along the correlation (p. 1125).

Read the disagreements as errors of the rectangle, with "in control" as the null hypothesis. A point inside the rectangle but outside the ellipse is fine for each variable alone but implausible for the pair, such as a high X with a low Y when the two always rise together: the rectangle misses the alarm, a Type II error. A point at a tip of the ellipse, beyond one limit, is unusual for one variable but exactly what the correlation predicts: the rectangle raises a false alarm, a Type I error.

The ellipse is the set of points at the same Mahalanobis distance from the centre, the distance of your Applied Statistics course that measures in units of the covariance:

\[ d^2(\vb{x}) = (\vb{x} - \vb{\mu})\T\, \Sigma^{-1}\, (\vb{x} - \vb{\mu}) \]
\(\vb{x}\)
the point: one value per variable
\(\vb{\mu}\)
the centre of the data
\(\Sigma\)
the covariance matrix

Hence the second idea of p. 1124, "Depth based control charts (nonparametric)": "map n-dimensional data to one dimension using depth e.g. Mahalanobis", "Build control charts for depth", "Compare against benchmark using depth e.g. Q-Q plots of depth of each data set". However many attributes a record has, its depth (how far inside the cloud it lies) is one number for an ordinary chart; if new data resemble the benchmark, the Q-Q plot of their depths follows the diagonal.

For signals, p. 1126 adds "Multiscale process control with wavelets": it "Detects abnormalities at multiple scales as large wavelet coefficients", is "Useful for data with heteroscedasticity" (a variance that changes over time) and was "Applied in chemical process control".

Models and baselines

"Model Fitting and Outliers" (p. 1127): "Models summarize general trends in data", "more complex than simple aggregates", like regressions that "focus on attribute relationships"; "Data points that do not conform to well fitting models are potential outliers" — potential, since the model may be wrong. Hence "Goodness of fit tests (DQ for analysis/mining)": "check suitableness of model to data", "verify validity of assumptions", "data rich enough to answer analysis/business question?". The residual analysis of Applied Statistics, turned on the data.

"Set Comparison and Outlier Detection" (p. 1128) works on whole data sets: the "“Model” consists of partition based summaries" (counts, quantiles, histograms per day or region), and you "Perform nonparametric statistical tests for a rapid section-wise comparison of two or more massive data sets"; "If there exists a baseline “good’’ data set, this technique can detect potentially corrupt sections in the test data set". A control chart for files, rapid because summaries are small.

Every method of this lesson, from the boxplot of the ages to the baseline comparison, does the same two things: it states what is expected, and it measures the departure.

A common way to handle missing values is to discard every record that contains one. Explain why the lecture warns against this policy. Support your answer with a computation: a table has 10 attributes, and each attribute is missing in 5 % of the records, independently of the others.

Show model answer

A record survives only if all of its attributes are present. With independent holes this happens with probability \((1 - 0.05)^{10} = 0.95^{10} \approx 0.60\), so about 40 % of the records would be discarded although only 5 % of each attribute is missing. The lecture reports that in federated data between 30 % and 70 % of the data points have at least one missing attribute, so ignoring them causes data wastage. Moreover, the remaining data is seriously biased: the complete records are representative only if values are missing completely at random, while in practice the holes often depend on the source or on the values themselves. The consequence is a lack of confidence in the results. Finally, the pattern of the missing data is itself useful, since it unearths data integrity issues. The alternative is imputation, keeping in mind that imputed values are useful in aggregates but cannot be trusted individually.

A complete answer names the slide's three consequences — wastage, bias, lack of confidence — and shows why 5 % becomes 40 %. Independence is part of the computation: state it.

Describe the techniques for imputing missing values presented in the lecture, in increasing order of sophistication, with the assumption each one makes. What does the lecture say about how far imputed values can be trusted?

Show model answer

1. Standalone imputation fills each hole with a point estimate of its attribute, such as the mean or the median; it assumes that the missing values are distributed like the non-missing ones, ignores the relationships between attributes and introduces bias, but it is convenient and easy to implement.

2. Better imputation uses the attribute relationships and assumes a monotone pattern: all prior attributes are populated. The regression method (parametric) sweeps left to right with linear regressions, X3 = a + b·X2 + c·X1, then X4 = d + e·X3 + f·X2 + g·X1, where a missing X3 is estimated from the first equation. The propensity score method (nonparametric) estimates the probability that Xj is missing from X1 … X(j−1) by logistic regression, groups the records by this score and fills the missing Xj within each group from the known ones with the approximate Bayesian bootstrap.

3. For an arbitrary missing pattern, Markov Chain Monte Carlo assumes multivariate Normal data with parameter Θ and alternates simulating the missing values given Θ and re-computing Θ from the filled-in data until stable; it is expensive and mostly used to induce monotonicity.

Imputed values are useful in aggregates but cannot be trusted individually.

What makes the answer complete is the order of the methods and the assumption of each. Monotonicity is easy to name and forget to explain: say what it means, "all prior attributes are populated".

Define an outlier as the lecture does. Should outliers be removed before an analysis? List the families of approaches the lecture gives for detecting outliers.

Show model answer

An outlier is a departure from the expected, so every detection approach is a way of defining what is expected. In the lecture's example, 3, 4, 7, 4, 8, 3, 9, 5, 7, 6, 92, the value 92 is suspicious. Outliers should not be removed automatically: they are potentially legitimate; often they are data or model glitches; but they could also be a data miner's dream, such as highly profitable customers. Each must be investigated with knowledge of the meaning of the data. The approaches are: error bounds and tolerance limits (control charts); model-based approaches (regression depth, analysis of residuals); geometric approaches; distributional approaches; and time-series outliers.

A complete answer has the definition in the slide's words, the three possible natures of an outlier and the five approaches. A sentence on why a plain 3-sigma rule can miss an outlier — the outlier inflates the standard deviation that judges it — shows understanding beyond the list.

Two positively correlated variables are monitored with a separate control chart each (component-wise limits). A point lies inside both univariate control bands but outside the ellipse of the bivariate Normal distribution. For the component-wise charts this point is:

  • a Type II error (a missed alarm) The point is out of control jointly, but each univariate chart accepts it: the alarm is missed.
  • a Type I error (a false alarm) A Type I error is an alarm raised for a point that is in control; here the component-wise charts raise no alarm at all.
  • correctly in control Outside the ellipse, the pair of values contradicts the correlation: the point is out of control.
  • correctly out of control The component-wise charts do not flag it, so they cannot be "correctly" signalling it.
Show model answer

A Type II error: the point is accepted by both component-wise limits, but it is out of control for the bivariate distribution, so the alarm is missed. The points at the tips of the ellipse that cross a univariate limit are the opposite case, Type I errors (false alarms). This is why the lecture says that component-wise limits lead to Type I and II errors.

A Shewhart control chart for kilogram calibrations prints its two control limits as "UCL = mean + 3s = −19.39 mg" and "LCL = mean − 3s = −19.57 mg". Compute s, in mg.

Show model answer

The two limits are 6s apart:

\[ s = \frac{-19.39 - (-19.57)}{6} \] \[ s = \frac{0.18}{6} = 0.03 \ \text{mg} \]

The mean is \((-19.39 - 19.57) / 2 = -19.48\) mg. The chart follows the main steps of a control chart: define the expected value and the departure (here the mean and 3s), compute the aggregate of each sample, plot the aggregates against the expected value and the error bounds, and declare "out of control" any point outside the bounds.

The only trap is the 6: the distance between the limits is twice 3s.

Key take-aways

  • Data munging means potentially lossy, not yet clear transformations; keep the raw file. No value is abnormal without the meaning of its column: 110 lies beyond the 102.5 fence, and only the header "Age(Years)" makes it abnormal.
  • Missing data is detected in two layers: overtly missing data (specifications, gaps, rough counts, estimates against expected values at several granularities) and hidden damage (censoring and truncation, defaults that look like values, errors of omission).
  • Values go missing at random (system failures), through wrong ingestion (merged and missing fields that shift columns) or by inapplicability (leave the slot empty); each kind has its own cure.
  • Dropping incomplete records wastes data and biases what remains: \((1 - q)^p\) turns 5 % holes in 10 attributes into 40 % of records lost. The imputation ladder runs from standalone estimates to regression and propensity scores under a monotone pattern, and to MCMC for any pattern; imputed values are useful in aggregates but not individually.
  • Censored data keeps the record and caps its value, a spike at the bound; truncated data drops the record, a dip. Metadata must record the existence and the nature of both, and spikes on default values reveal lost timestamps.
  • An outlier is a departure from the expected: legitimate, a glitch, or a data miner's dream. The classic z-score is masked, never above \((n-1)/\sqrt{n}\), so the 92 scores 3.006 while its robust z is 29.0.
  • Control charts define expected and departure, plot an aggregate per sample against the bounds and flag what leaves them. Component-wise limits on correlated variables give Type I and Type II errors; the ellipse, depth (Mahalanobis), wavelets, residuals and baseline comparisons extend the idea.
