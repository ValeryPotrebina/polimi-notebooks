Red pen: slips in the slides

113 places in the slides that the notebook corrects, module by module and in page order: what the slide prints, what is correct, and why.

The notebook is built from the course's own material: the professor's lecture slides and the decks of the exercise sessions. They are merged into one PDF of 1729 pages — the lecture decks on pages 1–1290, the exercise sessions on pages 1291–1729 — and every page number below is a page of that file, not the number printed on a slide.

Slides pack a lot into a few words, and here and there one of them states something that is not right: a command that would not run as printed, a reply the database would not give, a false "all" or "only", a picture that contradicts its own text. Wherever a lesson meets such a place, it marks it with a red pen. This page collects the red-pen notes of 38 lessons: 113 corrections on 110 pages of the slides.

The slides are the teacher's own material, and the exam may be matched against what they print. So learn both sides of every entry: the printed wording, to recognise it when a question quotes it, and the correct statement, to know what is true and to write it in your own answers. Many notes also say which wording to write and which to recognise.

Every entry has three parts: the quotation, exactly as printed; the correct statement; and the reason, with a check you can do yourself. Only real errors are listed — a different notation, an omitted detail or a clumsy sentence is not one. The reasons are copied from the lessons unchanged and sometimes point to what is around them there, a figure, a table or a widget: the link under each entry leads to that section of the lesson.

The entries are grouped by module, as everywhere in the notebook, and sorted by page inside each module; the row of page numbers under a module heading jumps straight to an entry.

"Terabytes to hexabyte of data cumulated on cheaper and cheaper storages"

The unit is the exabyte (EB, \(10^{18}\) bytes) — terabyte \(10^{12}\), petabyte \(10^{15}\), exabyte \(10^{18}\), zettabyte \(10^{21}\); "hexa-" is not an SI prefix.

The footnote of the chart on p. 15 gives the ladder: "1 petabyte = 1 million gigabytes. 1 zetabyte = 1 million petabytes". Each of those steps is \(10^6\), two units at a time: the terabyte sits between gigabyte and petabyte, and the exabyte, 1,000 petabytes, between petabyte and zettabyte. Write "exabyte" in an answer, but the exam may be matched against the slide's printed wording, so recognise "hexabyte" if a question uses it.

"Replication copies the entire database across all nodes in the distributed system."

Replication keeps several copies of the same data on different nodes. It need be neither the entire database nor on all nodes: real systems store each partition a fixed number of times — its replication factor, typically 3 — on some of the nodes.

The slides' own pictures do not fit the sentence. On p. 155 each of the six final nodes holds one piece, A, B or C — never the entire database — and each piece lives on two nodes of six, not on all; the HDFS slides of lesson 31 place the three replicas of a block on three chosen nodes (p. 857). Copying everything everywhere is one special case, and combined with partitioning it would undo the point of splitting. The exam may quote the slide's sentence: know it as printed, and give the precise version in an open answer.

Discussed in: lesson 3 · Transactions, partitions and replicas: what one machine promises, section "Split it, copy it: partitioning and replication"

"Graph DBs are better than relational!"

Graph databases are better than relational ones for relationship-centred data and queries: many-to-many links followed to a variable depth — paths, friends of friends, recommendations. That is what the bullet means under its heading "Relationship-based"; printed as a general verdict it is false. For set-oriented queries, aggregates over many rows, strict schemas and mature ACID tooling, a relational system remains the better fit.

In SQL every hop of a traversal is one more join, so a five-hop question costs five joins; a graph store follows stored relationships hop by hop. A total of kWh per hospital, instead, touches every row once and has no hop to save. The deck itself defends the relational model a few slides later: "Why didn't we change Relational? Consolidated, Integration platform, Granting ACID properties!" (p. 205, lesson 3). The exam may be matched against the printed wording, so know both the sentence as printed and the scope in which it holds.

"A distributed system can satisfy any two of these guarantees at the same time but not all three."

Partition tolerance is not one of the two you may decline. In a distributed system partitions will happen, so the theorem really says: while a partition lasts, choose consistency or availability. The next slide (p. 196) says so itself.

In 2012 Brewer himself wrote that the "2 of 3" formulation "was always misleading". The exam may quote the printed sentence: recognise it, and answer any question about choosing "CA" with p. 196.

"Soft State: abandon the consistency requirements of the ACID model pretty much completely"

Soft state means that the state of the system may change over time even without new input, because the replicas are still converging: what you read is not yet settled. What BASE gives up is ACID's immediate consistency at the end of every transaction, not consistency altogether — the E of BASE still promises convergence.

The printed line says what BASE drops when it is measured against ACID, not what the S names, and "pretty much completely" overstates it: in the very next bullet the same slide promises that "data will converge to a consistent state". In Fig. 4 replica 3 changes from 3 to 5 although nobody writes to it: that change without input is soft state. The exam may quote the printed sentence, so know it as printed, and give the precise meaning when you explain it.

"Hadoop, Cassandra, Hypertable"

Hadoop is not a database: it is the platform made of the HDFS file system and the MapReduce/YARN processing layer. Its column store is HBase, which the previous slide lists itself: "HBase (column-based)" (p. 211).

In the exam table "Hadoop" as a column DB names a file system and a batch engine, not a store that answers a query by key: write Cassandra or HBase. Lessons 31–32 show what Hadoop and HBase are. The printed list may still come back in a question, so know that the slide puts Hadoop there.

Discussed in: lesson 5 · CAP, BASE and the NoSQL landscape, section "Four families, one line each"

"Column data is saved together, as opposed to row data"

That sentence describes column-oriented storage — analytic engines and file formats such as Vertica, Parquet and ORC, where all the values of one column sit side by side for fast scans. Cassandra, HBase and Hypertable are wide-column (column-family) stores: rows are found by their key, and inside a column family the columns of one row are stored together with that key.

The course's own Cassandra lecture says "think of cassandra as row-oriented" (p. 417) and files Cassandra among the "Wide Column Stores" (p. 397); the course textbook, Kleppmann's, warns against calling the Bigtable model column-oriented. "Super useful for data analytics" is true of the first kind. The exam may echo the printed line; in a motivation for Cassandra write what it is built for: very large and growing volumes, written fast, read by key, scaled out by adding nodes.

"Mutual independence means that no non-key column is dependent upon any combination of the other columns"

Read it as "of the other non-key columns": no non-key column may depend on a combination of other non-key columns. The key columns are excluded, because every column depends on them by definition.

"Example: Publisher (Publisher_ID, Name, Address, City, State, Zip) Zip (Zip, City, State)"

"Has n(n-1) edges"

The complete undirected graph \(K_n\) has \(n(n-1)/2\) edges, one per unordered pair of distinct vertices. \(n(n-1)\) counts the ordered pairs: the arrows of a complete directed graph, where u → v and v → u are two edges.

Count the slide's own drawings: its \(K_4\) is a square with two diagonals, 6 edges, not 12; its \(K_5\) a pentagon with a pentagram inside, 10 edges, not 20. \(n(n-1)\) is also the sum of the degrees of \(K_n\): every edge counted from both ends. The course notes repeat the slip. Write \(n(n-1)/2\), but the exam may be matched against the printed wording: recognise \(n(n-1)\) as the slide's formula and know that it counts the directed case.

Discussed in: lesson 10 · Why graphs: property graphs, RDF and Neo4j, section "A graph, in the slides' words"

"Edges hold most of the important information and connect … nodes to properties"

In the property graph that Neo4j implements, a property is a key-value pair stored on a node or on a relationship; no edge leads to it. Edges — relationships — connect nodes to nodes.

The phrase describes the drawings of pp. 255–260, where every attribute is an oval at the end of an arrow: the triple style of RDF (next section), not Neo4j. The deck's own data-model slide puts the attributes on the nodes: "Nodes – with labels (type) and attributes" (p. 266). A theory question on property graphs and RDF tests exactly this difference, and it may be matched against the printed sentence: know the sentence, and know that it describes the triple style, not Neo4j.

"No Schemas", "No transactions", "No joins"

True of early MongoDB, not of today's. Schemas are optional, not absent (a collection can validate its documents), multi-document ACID transactions exist since version 4.0 (2018), and the $lookup aggregation stage joins two collections.

The course proves the last point itself: the paper of 30 January 2026 asks you to design "without using the MongoDB $lookup operation" — nobody bans an operation that does not exist. The idea behind the slide holds: embedding makes most joins and transactions unnecessary. The exam may be matched against the printed words, so know them; in an open answer, write that MongoDB avoids joins by embedding, not that it cannot join.

"Shard Key: {uId, regionCode}" — West: "MinKey, MinKey" to "MaxKey,50"; East: "MinKey, 50" to "MaxKey, MaxKey"; "Assign Regions 1-50 to the West"

With uId first, both printed ranges contain every user, whatever the region. To pin regions, the region must lead the key: shard key {regionCode, uId}, West from (MinKey, MinKey) to (51, MinKey), East from (51, MinKey) to (MaxKey, MaxKey).

"It just builds a lineage graph, which is an optimized execution plan."

The lineage graph records how each RDD derives from its parents. The execution plan — stages cut at the shuffles, one task per partition — is built from it by the DAG scheduler when an action runs. Optimising a plan, in the sense of rewriting a query, is what Catalyst does for DataFrames.

The same deck says what lineage is for: "Spark remembers how an RDD was built and can recompute lost partitions" (p. 1721). Nothing in an RDD's lineage is rearranged — your lambdas run as written; only consecutive narrow steps are fused into one task (Fig. 2).
