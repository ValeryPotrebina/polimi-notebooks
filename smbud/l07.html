Entity or attribute: two tests

Page 73 lists the "Crucial design choices" of conceptual design as three questions: "Should a concept be modeled as an entity or an attribute? Should a concept be modeled as an entity or a relationship? Identifying relationships: Binary or ternary? Aggregation?" The first two decide most of a diagram, and the lecture answers each with one example.

Take an employee and her address (p. 74). If every employee has one address and you only ever print it on an envelope, an attribute of Employee is enough. The slide names the two situations in which it is not:

  • "If we have several addresses per employee, address must be an entity (since attributes cannot be set-valued)."
  • "If the structure (city, street, etc.) is important, e.g., we want to retrieve employees in a given city, address must be modeled as an entity (since attribute values are atomic)."

Two tests: several values for one owner, or a structure you need to look inside — either one makes the concept an entity, linked to its owner by a relationship.

The car-rental exercise of the session applies both tests without naming them. The inspecting company is one name with nothing inside: the attribute Company Name (p. 1296). The licence's "list of vehicles they are allowed to drive" is several values, but a short fixed list of yes-or-no facts: the composite attribute Drives, one component per category — Truck, Cars, Bus, … (p. 1295).

"Structure forces an entity, so the museum's location (address and city) must be an entity." Not in the session's notation. The lecture's dialect (pp. 68–76) knows only atomic, single-valued attributes; the session adds the composite attribute, made of named parts, and draws exactly that location as one (p. 1306). Asked "attribute or entity?" at the exam, give the slide's two tests and say which one decides.

The same tests, on a relationship

The slide's text says Works_In4, its diamond Work_In4: one relationship, two spellings.

The tests bite harder on the attributes of a relationship (p. 75). Say Joe worked in the toy department from 2015 to 2016 and came back in 2019. Work_In4 links Employee and Department and carries from and to. The slide's verdict: "Works_In4 does not allow an employee to work in a department for two or more periods (a relationship is identified by participating entities)."

A relationship instance is the pair (Joe, Toys) and nothing more; from and to only describe it. A second period would need a second instance with the same pair — which is the same instance. The fix repeats the address case: "We want to record several values of the descriptive attributes for each instance of this relationship. Accomplished by introducing new entity set, Duration." Duration is identified by from and to together, Work_In4 becomes a ternary relationship among Employee, Department and Duration, and (Joe, Toys, 2015–2016) and (Joe, Toys, 2019–…) are two instances: the "Binary or ternary?" of p. 73, answered once.

Hold on to that sentence — "a relationship is identified by participating entities". It is the rule the dungeon table of the warm-up breaks.

Where an attribute lives

Page 76, "Entity vs. Relationship", asks the second question through a budget. Each manager receives a discretionary budget, dbudget, and the first diagram hangs it on the relationship Manages2, next to since. The slide approves that drawing for one reading only: "First ER diagram OK if a manager gets a separate discretionary budget for each dept."

Change one fact — "What if a manager gets a discretionary budget that covers all managed depts?" — and the same drawing goes wrong twice:

  • "Redundancy: dbudget stored for each dept managed by manager." A manager of three departments carries the same number in three Manages2 instances; update one, forget two, and the database contradicts itself.
  • "Misleading: Suggests dbudget associated with department-mgr combination." The diagram claims the budget depends on the pair, when it depends on the manager alone.

The fix, in the slide's box "This fixes the problem!", is a subclass: Manager ISA Employee carries dbudget, and Manages2 now links Manager and Department and keeps only since, which really does depend on the pair.

An attribute lives on the element whose instance alone determines its value; anywhere else it is copied, and misread.

Two remarks. The subclass has both "Reasons for using ISA" of p. 72 (lesson 6): "To add descriptive attributes specific to a subclass" — dbudget — and "To identify entities that participate in a relationship" — only managers manage. And the same question — where is this value determined? — is what moves the dungeon's multiplier in the last section.

A whole schema, and three ways to build one

Page 77, "E-R Example", is a complete schema in the course's own notation: the training company of the textbook the slides are taken from (Atzeni and colleagues — background, not on the slide). Nine entities, ten relationships, and every (min,max) pair sits next to the entity it counts: in how many instances of the relationship one instance of that entity takes part (lesson 6). Reading it aloud, relationship by relationship, is the drill every exam diagram needs.

A stray "(1,N)" also sits on the Surname stem of INSTRUCTOR. Nothing in the schema needs it: ignore it.

TELEPHONE is p. 74's first test in action: several numbers per instructor, so an entity and the relationship HOLDER. Four symbols of p. 77 come from the textbook, unexplained on the slides: a black dot is an identifier (Code of TRAINEE); an arc with one dot across Time, Room and Date of CLASS is a composite identifier — one room at one time on one date is one class; a dot on a bar across the line of EMPLOYMENT DATA is an external identifier — an EMPLOYEE record is identified through its trainee; a (0,1) on an attribute's stem makes it optional, like Marks. The exam papers list the attributes under the diagram and underline the keys; only the paper of 30 Jan 2026 draws them on the diagram, a filled dot for each key.

Three strategies

How does a designer get from a text to such a schema? Pages 78–83, "ER Design Methodology", answer with three pictures, two tables and no prose; the explanations below are the textbook's.

  • Top-down (p. 79): one abstract schema of the whole specification, the "Initial schema", refined through "Intermediate schema"s into the "Final schema"; each refinement applies a top-down primitive (p. 80).
  • Bottom-up (p. 81): the "Specifications" split into components and sub-components, a small schema for each, then the "Integration of schemas" into the "Final schema"; its primitives (p. 82) create concepts from nothing or from loose pieces.
  • Inside-out (p. 83): start from the most important concept — EMPLOYEE in the slide's example — and grow outward like an oil stain, each ring adding what is directly connected: PARTICIPATION and PROJECT, then MEMBERSHIP, MANAGEMENT and DEPARTMENT, then COMPOSITION and BRANCH.

Top-down T2, "From one entity to a generalization", is what Manager did to Employee. Top-down T4, "From one relationship to an entity with relationships", replaces a relationship by an entity of its own: the dungeon will need it in the last section. Duration was a different cure for the same illness: p. 75 keeps Work_In4 and adds a third participant.

"Top-down primitives" — the title printed over the table "Generation of an entity", "Generation of a relationship", "Generation of a generalization", "Aggregation of attributes on an entity", "Aggregation of attributes on a relationship".

These are the bottom-up primitives. The top-down ones are the six of p. 80.

Every row builds a schema up from nothing or from loose pieces — generation and aggregation — and the slide follows "Bottom-up strategy" (p. 81); every row of p. 80 refines a concept that already exists. The title was not updated. The exam is matched against the slides, so a question may use the printed title: know both what p. 82 prints and which strategy its table belongs to.

On a text the size of the session's exercises, inside-out keeps you oriented: start from the entity the first sentence is about, then follow the verbs outward.

What a diagram cannot say

Page 73 ends with a warning: "A lot of data semantics can (and should) be captured. But some constraints cannot be captured in ER diagrams." The exam papers write such constraints in a line under the diagram that starts with N.B. The restaurant paper (6 Sept 2024) draws Table 1:N — Serve — 1:N Waiter and adds "Tables are served by more Waiters overall, but only one at a time": the pairs count every instance the relationship ever holds, and no pair can say "at a time". The Netflix paper (21 Jan 2025) adds "Only the original digital content can be dubbed", a condition on an attribute value, not a count. The Formula 1 paper (11 Feb 2025) writes "Each Team Member might have worked for different Teams in different periods of time" — Joe's problem again.

From text to diagram: the car rental

Exercise 1 of the session (p. 1293) is a one-paragraph requirements text: "Design an ER Model for a car rental system that manages the customers, the cars and their related elements." Customers show an ID card, a driving licence and a credit card, cars are inspected, rentals produce invoices. Read the solution slides in order (pp. 1294–1298) and a method shows through; this notebook splits it into five passes over the text, each leaving its own mark:

  1. Nouns. Things with an identity of their own — with facts to keep or, like the customer, with links to everything else — become entities; p. 1294 prints them in capitals and colour.
  2. Attributes. The facts in parentheses, or after "described by", become attributes of the entity they describe.
  3. Keys and assumptions. Underline what identifies each entity; where the text gives nothing, invent an identifier and write the assumption next to it.
  4. Verbs and quantity words. A verb between two entities becomes a relationship; "each", "their", "the list of all" become (min,max) pairs.
  5. The process out. What the system does is not what it stores: drop the steps, keep only their data footprint.

Sweet shops only sell sweets. Food is described by the same attributes as gadgets, plus an expiry date."

Keep it or derive it?

The dungeon text (pp. 1310–1311) describes players, characters, items, weapons and monsters; its solution opens with two red remarks (p. 1312). "Do not forget the “Gold” attribute": the text only says gold "is provided to the player" when a monster is defeated, yet an amount that accumulates must live somewhere — the solution puts it on Character.

When a subclass earns its place

(1,1) next to Character says every character wields exactly one weapon. The text only bounds the maximum: write (0,1) if a character may be unarmed, and say so.

In Extra III a bug lets players "equip weapons regardless of the class of their character, but they can only equip one weapon", and a "Weapon Efficiency Score" gives each weapon a multiplier against misuse (p. 1319). Page 1320 draws one Wield for everybody, Character (1,1) — Wield — (0,N) Weapon, makes the score a composite attribute of Weapon with one component per class, circles both hierarchies and asks "Is it still necessary to keep the ISA Hierarchies?" — "They can be transformed into attributes". With their wielding rules gone, the classes have nothing of their own: they collapse into an attribute Class of Character, the weapon kinds into an attribute of Weapon.

Extra IV runs the argument backwards. Developers start to play; they "have the same attributes of players, although they have a special tag named “DEV” associated with their account" (p. 1321), so the slide draws a Developer subclass, "OR" a Developer attribute — both work, and the attribute is simpler. Then "allowing players to add developers to their friends is not a good practice" (p. 1322): one kind of player now has a relationship the other must not have.

A subclass earns its place with an attribute or a relationship of its own; without one, it is an attribute of the parent.

The structural fix has a price: Befriend now exists only among basic players, so developers cannot befriend each other either — more than the text asked. If that matters, it is exactly the kind of constraint that goes into an N.B. line.

Two more cases go through the same test. Boss Monster (p. 1313) fails it — no attribute or relationship of its own, only larger values — yet the slide keeps the subclass, (P,E); a Yes/No attribute would do as well. The museum's Extra III (p. 1307) passes it: "painters only befriend other painters and sculptors only befriend other sculptors". No solution is printed, but the device of p. 1322 gives one: Painter and Sculptor under Artist, (P,E) if other artists exist and nobody is both (state it), each with its own recursive Befriend.

Monster or fight? When a relationship must become an entity

Back to the warm-up. Extra I (p. 1316) introduces the "Killing Blow", which "awards the player that kills the monster with extra experience points, based on a “Monster Killing Blow Multiplier” score (between 1.2 and 1.5)". Exam-style questions

Theory questions in the form of the written exam — open answers of a few sentences, closed ones where a wrong answer may carry a penalty — and two exercises on ER fragments of past papers. In the nine past papers no exercise asks you to draw an ER diagram: every practical exercise starts from a given one, and its N.B. lines are where the design trouble hides.

Explain how you decide whether a concept should be modelled as an attribute or as an entity in an ER diagram. Use the address of an employee as the example.

Show model answer

It depends on how the information is used and on the semantics of the data. An attribute holds one atomic value for its owner. If an employee can have several addresses, address must be an entity connected to Employee by a relationship, because attributes cannot be set-valued. If the structure of the address matters — for example, we want to retrieve the employees who live in a given city — address must also be modelled as an entity, because attribute values are atomic. If neither holds (one address, always used as a whole), an attribute is enough. The same reasoning applies to attributes of relationships: since a relationship instance is identified by its participating entities, several values for the same pair (two working periods of one employee in one department) require a new entity, such as Duration.

A complete answer gives the two tests with their reasons — "set-valued" and "atomic" are the slide's words — and the decision for each case. The last sentence shows that you see the same rule behind p. 75.

True or false: in an ER diagram, a relationship Works_In between Employee and Department with the attributes from and to can record that the same employee worked in the same department in two different periods.

  • True The attributes describe one instance, and there is only one instance per (employee, department) pair.
  • False "A relationship is identified by participating entities" (p. 75): a second period of the same pair would be the same instance. The lecture adds an entity Duration and makes the relationship ternary.
Show model answer

False. A relationship instance is identified by its participating entities, so there is at most one instance for a given employee and department, with one value of from and to. Several periods require a new entity (Duration) and a ternary relationship.

A true/false with a possible penalty: the trap is that attributes look like they could repeat. They cannot — they hang on the pair.

Describe the top-down, bottom-up and inside-out strategies of conceptual design, and give one primitive of each of the first two.

Show model answer

Top-down: start from a single, abstract schema that covers the whole specification and refine it step by step — initial schema, intermediate schemas, final schema — each step replacing a concept with a more detailed structure; a primitive is "from one entity to two entities and a relationship between them" (others: to a generalization, from one relationship to multiple relationships or to an entity with relationships, adding attributes). Bottom-up: split the specification into components and sub-components, design a schema for each, then integrate the schemas into the final one; a primitive is "generation of an entity" (others: of a relationship, of a generalization, aggregation of attributes on an entity or a relationship). Inside-out: start from the most important concept and grow the schema outward, adding at each step the concepts directly connected to those already present, like an oil stain.

The slides give these strategies as pictures only (pp. 79–83), so explain the mechanism in your own words: refinement, integration, growth from a core. Remember that the bottom-up table is printed under the title "Top-down primitives".

Which of the following are primitives of the bottom-up strategy? Select all that apply.

  • Generation of a relationship Bottom-up T2 (p. 82).
  • Aggregation of attributes on an entity Bottom-up T4: loose attributes are gathered on an entity.
  • From one relationship to an entity with relationships Top-down T4 (p. 80): it refines an existing relationship.
  • Generation of a generalization Bottom-up T3.
  • From one entity to two entities and a relationship between them Top-down T1: it refines an existing entity.
Show model answer

Generation of a relationship, aggregation of attributes on an entity, generation of a generalization. The other two refine an existing concept and belong to the top-down strategy.

Rule of thumb: "generation" and "aggregation" build up (bottom-up); "from one … to …" refines (top-down). The bottom-up rows are printed under the title "Top-down primitives" (p. 82): know the printed title, and classify by the verbs.

In the role-playing-game exercise, a Character has Gold, Experience Points and a Strength Score derived from the strength of the equipped items. Which of these attributes would you keep in the ER model, and why?

Show model answer

An attribute whose value can be computed from data already stored is a derived attribute: storing it is redundant and can become inconsistent. Strength Score can be computed from the strength of the weapons the character wields, so it can be removed; it stays derivable even if it also depends on the Experience Points, since those are stored too. Experience Points can be derived under the assumption that they are only earned: they are then the sum of the experience provided by the monsters the character has defeated, which the model records. Gold must be kept under the assumption that it can be spent: spending is not recorded, so the current amount cannot be recomputed. The decision depends on these assumptions, which must be stated.

Three marks: the definition (derived = computable from stored data, hence redundant), one verdict per attribute, and the assumptions — the slides print both "Yes, they can be derived" and "No, they should be kept", each with its assumption.

On the ER diagram of the exam of 11 Feb 2025 (Formula 1 races). Consider this fragment of the diagram: Team Member 1:N — Include — 1:N Team, where Include has the attributes Hiring_Date and Resignation_Date, and the note "N.B. Each Team Member might have worked for different Teams in different periods of time." (a) State the two cardinalities in words. (b) Can the diagram record a mechanic who worked for the same team twice, in two different periods? (c) If not, change the conceptual design so that it can, and give the resulting entities, relationships and cardinalities.

Show model answer

(a) Each team member is included in at least one team and possibly many; each team includes at least one team member and possibly many.

(b) No. A relationship instance is identified by its participating entities, so Include contains at most one instance for the pair (mechanic, team), with one Hiring_Date and one Resignation_Date.

(c) Turn Include into an entity, Employment (Employment_ID, Hiring_Date, Resignation_Date), with Team Member (1,N) — Hold — (1,1) Employment and Employment (1,1) — In — (1,N) Team. Each period is now its own Employment instance, so the same member and team can appear in several periods. Equivalently, add a Period entity identified by Hiring_Date and Resignation_Date and make Include ternary, as the lecture does with Duration.

Part (b) is the point of the N.B.: the diagram alone cannot hold the situation the note describes. In (c), check that the new entity is (1,1) towards both old ones — every employment belongs to one member and one team. The paper itself does not ask (c); its MongoDB exercise asks for a documental representation of Team and of the team members, and that is where the N.B. bites: an array of periods inside each member can list the same team twice (lesson 13).

On the ER diagram of the exam of 6 Sept 2024 (restaurant). The diagram contains Table 1:N — Serve — 1:N Waiter, and the paper adds: "N.B. Tables are served by more Waiters overall, but only one at a time." Explain what the cardinalities express and why the paper needs the N.B. line.

Show model answer

Table 1:N means that each table is served by at least one waiter and possibly many; Waiter 1:N, that each waiter serves at least one table and possibly many. Cardinalities count all the instances of the relationship, whenever they hold, so they cannot say that at any given moment a table has only one waiter: that is a constraint over time. A lot of data semantics can be captured in an ER diagram, but some constraints cannot; they are stated in text, as in this N.B. line, and must be enforced by the database or by the application.

Two things make the answer complete: each pair read from its own side, and the link to p. 73, "some constraints cannot be captured in ER diagrams".

An instructor can have several telephone numbers, and each number belongs to one instructor. How does the schema of p. 77 model them?

  • As an attribute Phone of INSTRUCTOR An attribute holds one value; several numbers per owner fail the first test of p. 74.
  • As an entity TELEPHONE linked by HOLDER, with (1,1) next to TELEPHONE and (1,N) next to INSTRUCTOR Several values per owner make an entity. (1,1): each number has exactly one holder; (1,N): every instructor has at least one number.
  • As an entity TELEPHONE linked by HOLDER, with (1,N) next to TELEPHONE and (1,1) next to INSTRUCTOR The pairs are swapped: this says a number belongs to many instructors and an instructor has exactly one number.
  • As an attribute of the relationship TEACHING A number belongs to the instructor, not to an edition taught: wrong owner, and it would be repeated per edition.

Museum text: "Visitors state the time when they would visit the museum when they buy tickets." Ticket takes part in Buy with 1:1, Visitor with 1:N (p. 1306). Where can Visiting Time go without losing or repeating anything? Select all that apply.

  • On the relationship Buy The value is fixed by the purchase — where the slide puts it.
  • On Ticket Each ticket has exactly one purchase (1:1 on its side), so the attribute can move to Ticket without loss.
  • On Visitor A visitor buys several tickets with different times: one value per visitor would lose them.
  • On Museum A museum sells many tickets: its one value would be nobody's visiting time.

The (1,1) side can absorb a relationship's attributes — the same fact that, in lesson 8, lets a key travel into that table.

A manager's discretionary budget covers all the departments they manage. What is wrong with keeping dbudget on Manages2 (p. 76)?

  • The same value is stored once per managed department, and it suggests the budget depends on the manager–department pair The slide's two words: "Redundancy" and "Misleading". The fix moves it onto a subclass Manager.
  • Relationships cannot have attributes They can: since stays on Manages2, because it does depend on the pair.
  • A department could then have two managers "At most one manager" is a cardinality of Manages2; where dbudget sits does not change it.
  • dbudget would become part of the key of Manages2 A relationship is identified by its participants; descriptive attributes never join its identity.

Car-rental text. Which fragments leave no trace in the ER model? Select all that apply.

  • "the keys are provided to the customer" An action, not a fact to store — and car keys, not identifiers.
  • "the list of cars is shown to the customer" Showing is a query over Car; nothing new is stored.
  • "the system checks whether a car inspection has been performed in the last 3 months" A query over the inspections' Date; "3 months" appears nowhere in the model.

In Extra II the Killing Blow multiplier depends on how well the player fought. Why does the slide move it off Monster?

Key take-aways

  • Entity or attribute: several values for one owner, or a structure you need to look inside, make a concept an entity (p. 74); a short fixed list or a set of named parts can stay a composite attribute in the session's notation.
  • A relationship instance is identified by its participants: when the same pair must carry several values — two working periods, three killing blows — each repetition needs an identity of its own, through a third participant (Duration, p. 75) or by turning the relationship into an entity (a Killing Blow entity: top-down primitive T4, reification).
  • An attribute lives on the element whose instance alone determines its value; anywhere else it is redundant and misleading (dbudget moves to Manager, p. 76; the KB Multiplier sits on Monster in Extra I and on the fight in Extra II).
  • Designers work top-down (refinement, primitives T1–T6), bottom-up (components, schemas, integration; T1–T5 on the slide mistitled "Top-down primitives") or inside-out (an oil stain from the central concept); constraints a diagram cannot express go into N.B. lines.
  • From text to diagram: nouns, attributes, keys with written assumptions, verbs and quantity words, then drop the process and keep its footprint. Derived attributes go when they can be recomputed under a stated assumption; a subclass stays only with an attribute or a relationship of its own.
