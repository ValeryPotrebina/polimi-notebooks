Search is not a WHERE clause: relevance, TF-IDF and the inverted index

After this lesson you will turn the lecture's eight book titles into TF-IDF vectors by hand, rebuild its ranking down to the score 0.159, say why a title can score exactly zero, and explain how an inverted index answers a query in tenths of a second.

Eight books, known only by their titles (p. 557). A customer is interested in Building data mining applications for CRM, and the system recommends the closest of the other seven (p. 564). First comes Data Mining Your Website. Third comes Mastering Data Mining: The Art and Science of Customer Relationship Management — although it shares with the query exactly the same two words as the winner: data and mining.

Further down, Customer knowledge management is "Not recommended": its score is exactly 0. Yet CRM stands for customer relationship management, and the third title spells those three words out. They earn it nothing.

Why do the same two words count for more in the shorter title, and why can the system not see what CRM means? By the end of the lesson you will compute the whole ranking by hand — 0.159, 0.102, 0.094 and four zeros — and answer both.

A query that answers "how well"

Number the eight titles in the slide's order (p. 557); T1 is the book the customer likes.

Ask a relational database for the books about data and you write a predicate:

-- T1, T3 and T4: a set, in no order
SELECT title FROM books WHERE LOWER(title) LIKE '%data%';

Each row satisfies the WHERE clause or not, so T1, T3 and T4 come back as a set: none is "more about data" than the others, and a title that says the same in other words is left out. Right for an invoice; wrong for "books like this one".

The lecture starts from this gap. After recalling the four NoSQL families of the course — document, graph, key-value, wide-column (p. 551) — its roadmap slide (p. 552) lists the "New ingredients" that search adds to database access: intent, similarity, relevance, ranking and, in brackets, efficiency.

Intent: what the user really wants

A query is a few words; behind it there is a need. The slide defines intent as "the underlying goal, purpose, need, or desire a user has when interacting with a system (e.g., entering a search query or a message to a chatbot)" and names four kinds (p. 553):

Matching, similarity, relevance, ranking

Matching is "the technical process of linking the user's input (query, utterance) to predefined intents or data within the DB" (p. 554), and the slide's title, "Matching (→ Similarity?)", hints at how: by computing how similar the input is to each item. Similarity is a number the system computes.

Relevance is not. The same slide calls it "the qualitative measure of how well the search results align with the user's actual needs and expectations, ultimately satisfying their intent", and gives a test: "A result is relevant if the user finds what they were looking for quickly and easily". Ranking sorts the results by similarity, best first; efficiency keeps it all fast (section 5).

Similarity is computed by the system; relevance is judged by the user. A ranking is the system's bet that the first predicts the second.

What is compared: attributes or text

To compute a similarity you need the content of an item (p. 555): explicit attributes — for a film, "Action / adventure", "Feature Bruce Willis", "Year 1995" — which compare like columns, or textual content, "title, description, table of content, etc.", for which "Several techniques exist to compute the distance between two textual documents". The rest of the lecture builds one of them.

The slide files the book example under information filtering (p. 557) — keeping and ordering the items that match one user's interest — and calls it "An (unrealistic) example", matching "based only on their title": small enough to compute by hand.

Then comes the "Problem: importance of words" (p. 556). In raw counts "the" weighs as much as "mining", a word three titles share as much as a word only one title has, and a long title overlaps with anything by sheer bulk. Three questions, three fixes: how loud is the term in this document (TF), how rare in the collection (IDF), how long is the document (normalisation).

Loud here, rare elsewhere: TF × IDF

Page 559 gives the plan: "Each document is transformed into a normed TFIDF vector, size N" — one coordinate per term of the vocabulary, N = 29 here, most of them 0. Each non-zero coordinate is the product of two factors.

TF: how loud the term is in this document

Term frequency is local, one document at a time. Page 560 defines it as a fraction:

\[ \mathrm{tf}_{i,j} = \frac{n_{i,j}}{|d_j|} \]
\(n_{i,j}\)
number of times term \(i\) appears in document \(j\)
\(|d_j|\)
number of terms in document \(j\)

The "Actual formula" of p. 562 uses a different one:

\[ TF = \log(\mathit{count} + 1) \]

Both are standard. The fraction makes an occurrence count less in a longer document; the logarithm damps repetition — a term seen twice is not twice as telling: \(\log 3 = 1.099\) against \(\log 2 = 0.693\). Natural logarithms throughout; another base multiplies every weight by one constant, which the normalisation below removes.

IDF: how rare the term is in the collection

Inverse document frequency is global. Page 561 calls it a measure of "how common a term is across the collection of documents, the lower the less important that term is": the more documents contain the term, the lower its IDF.

\[ \mathrm{idf}_i = \log \frac{|D|}{|\{d : i \in d\}|} \]
\(|D|\)
number of documents in the collection
\(|\{d : i \in d\}|\)
number of documents containing term \(i\): its document frequency, df
The hyphens in #docs-the-term-occurs-in join one name; they are not minus signs. It is df.

The "Actual formula" of p. 562 differs again, slightly:

\[ IDF = \log\left( \frac{\#\mathit{docs} + 1}{\begin{gathered} \#\mathit{docs}\text{-}\mathit{the}\text{-}\mathit{term}\text{-} \\ \mathit{occurs}\text{-}\mathit{in} \end{gathered}} \right) \]

The +1 matters at the edge: a term in all eight titles gets \(\log(8/8) = 0\) from p. 561 but \(\log(9/8) = 0.118\) from p. 562 — still the weakest, never exactly zero, so no document becomes a vector of zeros that cannot be normalised. Here df is 1, 2 or 3, so IDF takes three values:

Rarity is measured in this collection, not in English: "a" and "the" occur in one title each, so they get the top IDF, 2.197, like "website". The weight of a term is the product of the two factors — loud here times rare elsewhere (p. 561):

\[ (\text{tf-idf})_{i,j} = \mathrm{tf}_{i,j} \times \mathrm{idf}_i \]

Page 560 says what the product is for: it "Measures the relevance of a term inside a document" and "Penalizes the words that appear too often across different documents". Relevance in a loose sense here: the weight of a term, not the user's judgement of p. 554.

IDF comes from Karen Spärck Jones, in a short 1972 paper in the Journal of Documentation: terms "should be weighted according to collection frequency, so that matches on less frequent, more specific, terms are of greater value than matches on frequent terms".

"(Term Frequency / Inverted Document Frequency)"

Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency, as p. 560 of the same deck prints it; p. 561 too says "Inverse Document Frequency".

"Inverse" says that the factor grows as the document frequency shrinks, \(\log(|D| / \mathrm{df})\); "inverted" is the name of the index of section 5, a different thing. The slash only separates the two names, as in the common spelling TF/IDF: the weight is their product, \(\mathrm{tf} \times \mathrm{idf}\) (p. 561). The exam may be matched against the printed wording: recognise "inverted" there, and write "inverse" yourself.

Normalisation: every document gets length 1

The last fix is length. The formula of p. 559, boxed in red on p. 562, divides every weight of a document by the length of its whole vector:

Here N counts the terms. Textbooks often use N for the number of documents, which these slides write \(|D|\) or #docs.
\[ TFIDF = \frac{TF \times IDF}{\sqrt{\sum_N (TF \times IDF)^2}} \]
\(TF \times IDF\)
the raw weight of one term in this document
\(\sum_N\)
sum over the N terms of the vocabulary (here 29), for the same document
\(\sqrt{\cdots}\)
the Euclidean length (norm) of the document's raw vector

This normalisation makes every document a vector of length 1. Long titles lose their bulk advantage, and the same raw weight ends up lighter in a long title, which shares the unit length among more terms — why p. 563 circles two different numbers for one word (Figure 3).

From vectors to a ranking

The third line of p. 559 says "The distance between any pair of vectors is computed". Which distance? For unit vectors the Euclidean distance and the cosine similarity give the same ranking — four short steps show why.

  1. Write the squared distance between the query \(\vb{q}\) and a document \(\vb{d}\) as a dot product:

    \[ \lVert \vb{q} - \vb{d} \rVert^2 = (\vb{q} - \vb{d}) \cdot (\vb{q} - \vb{d}) \]

  2. Expand it as you would \((a - b)^2\):

    \[ = \vb{q} \cdot \vb{q} - 2\, \vb{q} \cdot \vb{d} + \vb{d} \cdot \vb{d} \]

  3. Both vectors are normed, so \(\vb{q} \cdot \vb{q} = \vb{d} \cdot \vb{d} = 1\); and for unit vectors the dot product is the cosine of the angle between them:

    \[ \lVert \vb{q} - \vb{d} \rVert^2 = 2 - 2 \cos\theta \]

  4. The distance falls exactly when the cosine rises. Ranking by the smallest distance or by the largest cosine gives the same order, and the one number to compute is \(\vb{q} \cdot \vb{d}\).

The same identity from Applied Statistics: on rows scaled to length 1, the Euclidean distance and the cosine distance \(1 - \cos\theta\) rank any pairs alike.

So the score is the dot product, and for sparse vectors it is short: only terms both documents contain survive, every other product has a 0 in it. T1 and T4 share data and mining, each contributing \(0.251 \times 0.316\):

\[ \begin{aligned} s(T_1, T_4) &= 2 \times (0.251 \times 0.316) \\ &= 0.159 \end{aligned} \]

Back to the puzzle

Both questions of the opening now have answers. The same two words count for more in T4 because normalisation spreads each title's unit length over its terms, four in T4 and eleven in T3: 0.316 against 0.187 per word, 0.159 against 0.094 in total.

And CRM stays invisible because the system compares tokens, not meanings: "crm" and "customer relationship management" are different strings. T3's three spelled-out words earn it nothing, and T8's score is a sum over an empty set, exactly 0. Type the expansion into the lab and the picture turns over — T8 rises to #1 with 0.503, and the book that says "CRM" drops to 0 (Figure 5).

Seeing that two strings mean the same thing needs a representation of meaning: embeddings, which place related texts close together whatever their spelling (lesson 27). If you met word embeddings in AN2DL, this is where they come back — compared with the same cosine.

A lexical score is a sum over the shared terms: no shared token, no score — whatever the meaning.

What a real engine computes

Page 560 opens by saying where TF-IDF is used: in the scoring function of Lucene, the search library inside Elasticsearch. The idea is right; the name is out of date.

"Results are scored with Lucene’s Practical Scoring Function, which uses TF-IDF"

Since Elasticsearch 5.0 (October 2016) the default similarity is BM25, which keeps the TF and IDF ideas but weighs them differently. The practical scoring function was the default only up to the 2.x versions.

The 5.0 release announcement: "The default relevance calculation has been changed from TF/IDF to the more modern BM25". BM25 still makes rare terms weigh more and long fields less, but lets term frequency saturate: a tenth repetition adds far less than a second. The exam may be matched against the printed wording: asked what the slides say, answer TF-IDF and Lucene's practical scoring function; know that Elasticsearch now computes BM25.

In Elasticsearch you never compute these weights yourself: a match query (lesson 26) returns the titles that share a term with the query, sorted by BM25 score — T8 would not even be listed.

Under the hood: an index turned inside out

So far you looked at every title. "On the internet, time affects attention", p. 565 warns: "attention is money", and "search engines need to respond in tenths of a second". Reading every document at query time is out of the question; the answer must be prepared before the query arrives.

The preparation is the inverted index, "the building blocks of search engines", "made of mappings: TermIDs => DocumentIDs" (p. 566). It is the index at the back of a textbook: you do not read four hundred pages to find "mitochondria", you look the word up and get a list of pages.

Inverted index — for every term, the list of the documents that contain it, called its posting list. "Inverted" because a document naturally lists its words; the index lists, for each word, its documents.

Page 568 builds one from "The quick brown fox jumped over the lazy dog" and "Quick brown foxes leap over lazy dogs in summer". The index "Lists every unique word from every document and identifies which documents a word appears in": "quick" -> D1,D2, "fox" -> D1, "summer" -> D2… Two entries reveal what the slide does not say. "quick" -> D1,D2, though D2 writes Quick: the index lower-cased. "fox" -> D1 only, though D2 has foxes: no stemming, so foxes is another term. An index finds exactly the token it stored, so the query must be cut the same way — what Elasticsearch calls analysis (lesson 25).

A query is a join of posting lists

"Computing retrieval function involves: finding all documents containing set of terms, i.e. computing a join over the posting lists" (p. 567). For "quick fox" the engine intersects two short lists, D1, D2 and D1: the answer is D1 — in SQL terms, a join on the document id. With both lists sorted by document id, one pass over each suffices, and no document is opened (background, not on the slide). OR takes the union; only its documents get scored.

Small enough to stay in memory

The lists must be small and quick to read. Page 566: they are "compressed with integer compression algorithms that allow for fast decompression, take statistics of text into account, are in blocks to allow for easy reading" — and "why compressed? to reduce the amount of space in memory". An index that fits in RAM is read at memory speed, and decompressing a block costs less than fetching it from disk.

One common trick (background, not on the slide) shows what "statistics of text" can mean: store a sorted id list as the gaps between ids. A frequent term's ids lie close together, so the gaps are small numbers needing few bits (Figure 6, left).

Stopping early

Page 567 adds a second speed-up: "Entries in the posting list are sorted from most important documents (with highest term counts) to least", which "allows for early termination". The engine computes "bounds on scores" — the best score an unread document could still reach — and when that bound cannot beat the results found, it skips the rest: "lower ranked documents dropped as early as possible". The last bullet adds index pruning techniques, which "get rid of documents that would never be retrieved for a certain query": their entries are never read at all. The top 10 of a billion cost a small fraction of the billion.

Sorted by importance, or by document id?

Document-id order gives small gaps and one-pass intersections; importance order brings the best documents first. Lucene, inside Elasticsearch, keeps document-id order and stores per block the best score a document in it could reach, skipping blocks that cannot reach the top — both ideas at once (background, not on the slide). At the exam write the slide's version, knowing it is one of two layouts.

The inverted index answers from term lists, never from the documents: the lists are joined, compressed to stay in memory, and ordered so that the engine can stop early.

The third. A user searching "best laptop for students" is considering a purchase and wants to compare options or research products: commercial/investigational intent. True or false: with the normed TF-IDF vectors of the lecture, ranking documents by their Euclidean distance from the query vector gives the same order as ranking them by cosine similarity.

  • True For unit vectors the squared distance is 2 − 2 cos θ: it shrinks exactly when the cosine grows.
  • False That would hold for raw vectors of different lengths, where a long document is far from everything; normalisation removes the length.
Show model answer

True. After normalisation every vector has length 1, so

\[ \lVert \vb{q} - \vb{d} \rVert^2 = \vb{q} \cdot \vb{q} - 2\, \vb{q} \cdot \vb{d} + \vb{d} \cdot \vb{d} \] \[ = 2 - 2\cos\theta . \]

A smaller distance means a larger cosine, so both criteria give the same ranking, and the score to compute is the dot product of the normed vectors.

If the question comes as an open one, write the one-line derivation, not only the verdict. Without normalisation the two orders can differ: a long document is far from every short query even when it points the same way.

Consider the Dish entity of the restaurant ER diagram of the exam of 6 Sept 2024: Dish (Dish_ID, Name, Price, Recipe, Description). Three dishes are named D1 "grilled salmon", D2 "salmon tartare with avocado" and D3 "grilled octopus". Treat each name as a document (lower-case, no stemming, every word kept) and use the formulas of the lecture: TF = log(count + 1), IDF = log((#docs + 1) / #docs-the-term-occurs-in), then normalise each vector to length 1. Which dish is the most similar to D1? Enter its similarity score with three decimals.

Show model answer

#docs = 3. df: grilled 2, salmon 2, tartare 1, with 1, avocado 1, octopus 1. IDF: df 2 gives log(4/2) = log 2, df 1 gives log 4 = 2 log 2. Every count is 1, so TF = log 2 for every term, and each raw weight is (log 2)² times 1 (df 2) or times 2 (df 1); the common factor cancels in the normalisation.

D1 is proportional to (grilled 1, salmon 1): 0.707 each. D2 is proportional to (salmon 1, tartare 2, with 2, avocado 2), length √13: salmon 0.277. D3 is proportional to (grilled 1, octopus 2), length √5: grilled 0.447.

sim(D1, D2) = 0.707 × 0.277 = 0.196; sim(D1, D3) = 0.707 × 0.447 = 0.316. D3 is the most similar, with 0.316.

Using the formulas of the lecture (TF = log(count + 1), IDF = log((#docs + 1) / #docs-the-term-occurs-in), then normalisation), explain why the word "data" has weight 0.316 in the title "Data Mining Your Website" but 0.187 in "Mastering Data Mining: The Art and Science of Customer Relationship Management". What would the second weight become if the word "the" were removed from that title?

Show model answer

In both titles "data" occurs once, so TF = log 2, and it occurs in 3 of the 8 titles, so IDF = log(9/3) = log 3: the raw weight is the same, log 2 × log 3 = 0.7615. The difference comes from the normalisation, which divides each weight by the Euclidean length of the whole title vector: 2.408 for the four-term title, 4.074 for the eleven-term one. So 0.7615 / 2.408 = 0.316 and 0.7615 / 4.074 = 0.187: the same word counts for more in a short document. Removing "the" removes one raw weight of 1.523 from the sum under the square root: the length drops to √(16.598 − 2.320) = 3.779, and "data" rises to 0.7615 / 3.779 = 0.202, although its TF and IDF do not change.

Three things to say: same TF, same IDF, different length. The trap in the second part is to think the weight of "data" cannot change because nothing about "data" changed — normalisation couples every word of a document.

What is an inverted index? Explain how a search engine uses it to answer a query with several terms, and why, according to the lecture, its posting lists are compressed and sorted from the most important documents to the least.

Show model answer

An inverted index is the building block of a search engine: a mapping from each term (TermID) to the list of the documents that contain it (DocumentIDs), its posting list. It is called inverted because it turns the natural direction, document to words, into word to documents. To answer a query with several terms, the engine finds all documents containing the set of terms by computing a join over their posting lists — the intersection for AND, the union for OR — without reading the documents. Search engines must respond in tenths of a second, so the lists are compressed with integer compression algorithms that allow fast decompression, take the statistics of text into account and are organised in blocks for easy reading; the reason is to reduce the space in memory, so that the index is kept and read in RAM. Sorting each posting list from the most important documents (highest term counts) to the least allows early termination: the engine computes bounds on the scores that the remaining documents could reach and drops lower-ranked documents as early as possible, while index pruning removes entries that would never be retrieved for a query.

You delete the word "the" from T3 and recompute the normed vectors. What happens to the weight of "data" in T3?

  • It stays 0.187: the count and the IDF of "data" did not change TF and IDF are unchanged, but the length of T3's vector shrinks, and every weight is divided by that length.
  • It grows, to about 0.202 One raw weight fewer under the square root: the length falls from 4.074 to 3.779, and 0.7615 / 3.779 = 0.202.
  • It drops, because T3 now has fewer words in common with T1 A weight inside T3 does not depend on T1 at all; and T1 never contained "the".
  • It becomes 0.316, as in T4 T3 still has ten weighted terms against T4's four, so its length stays larger.

  • 0 with both formulas: a word in every document carries no information That is p. 561's value only, log(8/8) = 0. The +1 of p. 562 gives log(9/8).
  • 2.197 with both, the highest IDF on the shelf 2.197 = log 9 belongs to a term in a single title; "book" is in all eight.
  • 0.118 with p. 562's formula and 0 with p. 561's log(9/8) = 0.118 and log(8/8) = 0: the +1 keeps a universal term small but never exactly zero.
  • 1 with both, since it occurs in 8 of 8 titles IDF is the logarithm of a ratio, not a frequency; 8 of 8 gives log 1 = 0 in p. 561's formula.

T3 and T4 share exactly the same two words with the query, data and mining. Why does T4 score 0.159 and T3 only 0.094?

  • "data" and "mining" occur more often in T4 Once each, in both titles.
  • Their IDF is higher in T4 IDF belongs to the term, not to the document: log 3 in both titles.
  • T3 also shares "customer" and "relationship" with the query, which lowers its score The query T1 contains neither word; and with non-negative weights an extra shared term could only add to a dot product.
  • T4 is shorter, so after normalisation each of its terms keeps a larger share of the length 1 Lengths 2.408 against 4.074, hence 0.316 against 0.187 per word.

Why does the lecture compress posting lists (p. 566)?

  • To hide their content from whoever reads the disk Compression is not encryption.
  • To save disk space; reading them gets slower, but that is the price The slide lists compression under "engineered to be fast", with codes chosen for fast decompression.
  • To reduce the amount of space in memory, so the index can be kept and read in RAM "why compressed? to reduce the amount of space in memory" — and memory is what makes tenths of a second possible.
  • Because posting lists are stored as text They store integers — document ids — hence "integer compression algorithms".

A posting list is sorted from the documents with the highest term counts to the lowest (p. 567). How does that let the engine stop early when it needs only the top 10?

  • It reads the first 10 entries and stops, whatever their scores With several query terms a document can score high through another term; the engine stops when a bound says nothing left can enter the top 10, not after a fixed count.
  • It keeps a bound on the best score the unread documents could still reach, and stops when that bound falls below the 10th best score found "compute bounds on scores", "lower ranked documents dropped as early as possible".
  • It deletes the low-count entries from the index while answering Nothing is deleted while a query runs: early termination only skips the rest of the list for this query. Getting rid of entries for good is index pruning, the last bullet of p. 567.
  • It reads the list backwards, from the least important documents The order exists so that the most important entries come first.

Key take-aways

  • A WHERE clause decides in or out; search scores and ranks. Its new ingredients (p. 552) are intent, similarity, relevance, ranking and efficiency; similarity is computed by the system, relevance is judged by the user.
  • Intent comes in four kinds (p. 553): informational, navigational, commercial/investigational and transactional.
  • A text becomes a bag of words — scanned, parsed, counted, maybe stemmed. TF says how loud a term is in a document, IDF how rare it is in the collection, and their product weighs the term; the table of p. 563 uses TF = log(count + 1) and IDF = log((#docs + 1) / df), with every stop word kept.
  • Normalisation divides each document by its length, so every document is a unit vector: distance and cosine rank alike, and the score is a dot product over the shared terms — 0.159, 0.102 and 0.094 for T4, T2 and T3, and 0 for the rest.
  • Lexical matching cannot see synonyms or abbreviations: no shared token, score 0. That is why T8 scores 0 against a book about CRM, and why meaning needs embeddings.
  • An inverted index maps each term to its posting list; a query is a join of posting lists; the lists are compressed to stay in memory and ordered by importance for early termination.
  • Three corrections to carry: "inverse", not "inverted", document frequency; the example keeps the stop words that p. 559 says are dropped; current Elasticsearch scores with BM25, not the practical scoring function of p. 560.
