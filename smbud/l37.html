Not all tables are created equal: tidy data, keys, duplicates and tools

After this lesson you will spot the four messy shapes of a table and repair each one, explain why matching identities and finding duplicates works only to a degree of accuracy, say why schema on read makes wrangling the reader's job, and name the tools that record a wrangling job as replayable steps.

Page 1133 of the slides prints a small table, Year | Comedy-Q1 | Thriller-Q1 | Action-Q1 | …, with 2 comedy hits in Q1 2014 and none in Q1 2015. Under it, one question — "Find total comedy movies in all of 2014?" — and the slide's verdict: "Not easy in current form".

Not easy? With only the Q1 columns printed, you can read the answer off the table: 2. But the "…" says that more columns follow — Comedy-Q2, Comedy-Q3, Comedy-Q4, one per category and quarter. Now write the query that stays right as they arrive. You cannot: you would need to know today which columns the table will have tomorrow. The thing you want to filter on, the quarter, is in no cell at all. It is hiding inside the column names.

The same six numbers, laid out differently, make the question one filter and one sum. By the end of the lesson you will know the rule the wide table breaks, the move that repairs it, and why in a big-data system it is you, the reader, who has to make that move — not the database.

Before the table: what kind of value, from which source

Wrangling starts before any table exists, with two questions. What kind of value is this? And should I trust the place it comes from? The slides answer the first with a bunch of balloons (p. 1130).

Read the bunch as five facts about one object. Its colour is categorical: a label taken from a set of possible values. "Nice, Good, For birthday" are qualitative, and the slide adds "Subjective": an opinion that depends on who gives it. "6 balloons", "Pressure 15 PSI" and "139 m. over sea level" are quantitative. The first is discrete, a count with gaps between its values; the other two are continuous, measurements on a scale limited only by the instrument.

Why sort values at all? Because the type decides which cleaning makes sense. You can average a pressure, not a colour. "6.5 balloons" is a glitch, "15.3 PSI" is not. "Nice" needs text handling, not statistics. And only a quantitative value can sit outside the whiskers of a boxplot, as an outlier (lesson 36).

"Categorical and qualitative are the same thing." In Applied Statistics they were: both meant non-numeric variables, nominal or ordinal. The slide separates them — categorical for labels such as a colour, qualitative for subjective judgements such as "Nice". At the exam, reproduce the slide's three groups with its examples.

The second question gets twelve words on p. 1131, the data source selection criteria. The slide prints them in two columns without comment. The table groups them by the question each one asks; the grouping and the questions are ours, the words are the slide's.

Suppose you need hit counts per film category and quarter, the data of the next section. A chart scraped from a fan site is accessible, free and current, but its credibility and verifiability are low and its legal status is unclear (scraping is lesson 34). An industry feed is credible and verifiable, but it costs money. No source wins on all twelve. The list makes the trade-off explicit before you spend a week cleaning the wrong data.

Currency means up-to-dateness, not money — money is Cost. A closed question can hide exactly that trap.

Observations and variables: the shape of a table

Page 1132 shows the same six numbers in two tables: the student counts of two schools in three years.

The slide labels the first table with the words of a spreadsheet, Column and Row, and the second with the words of the data, Variable and Observation. That difference is the whole lesson.

Variable — a property measured on every unit: school, year, student count. Observation — all the values measured on one unit: one school in one year. Rows and columns are where values sit; observations and variables are what they mean.

In the wide table the variable year has no column. Its values 2012, 2013 and 2014 are column names, and each row carries three observations. In the long table each variable is one column and each row one observation. Same information, different shape — and the shape decides which questions are easy.

Page 1133 makes it worse. Its header Comedy-Q1 glues two values into one name, a category and a quarter, and the "…" promises Comedy-Q2, Thriller-Q2 and more. Here is the hook's question against the wide table and against the long table of the same slide, Category | Quarter | Year | #Hits:

-- wide table: returns 2
-- (the one comedy column of Q1)
SELECT "Comedy-Q1"
FROM hits
WHERE Year = 2014;
-- long table: returns 2
-- (one filter, one sum)
SELECT SUM("#Hits")
FROM hits
WHERE Category = 'Comedy'
  AND Year = 2014;

Both return 2 today. Only the second is still right when Q2 arrives. The first must be rewritten for every new quarter, because what it filters on lives in the column names. The slide's second note, "Very easy to add a new column", says the same from the other side: a new measure — say the number of movies released, which its "% of hit comedy movies" question would need — is one more column in the long table, but a whole block of new columns in the wide one.

The repair has a name. Melt (also called pivot longer) turns the headers that are values into a column, one row per old cell. Its inverse, pivot (pivot wider), spreads the values of a column into headers. In pandas, which p. 1143 lists among the wrangler's tools, the melt and the split of the glued header are two lines; R's tidyr (p. 1149) calls the moves pivot_longer and pivot_wider.

# wide -> long: 6 rows,
# one per (Year, old header)
long = wide.melt(
    id_vars="Year",
    var_name="Header",
    value_name="#Hits")
# split the glued header in two
long[["Category", "Quarter"]] = (
    long["Header"]
    .str.split("-", expand=True))

Page 1134 holds the messiest case, "Variables in both rows and columns". The quarters run across the headers (Q1, Q2, Q3, …) and the rating runs down the rows: an Excellent row and a Good row for each category. One observation, a category in a quarter, is smeared over two rows and several columns.     Step 3. Excellent and Good are values. Why may they be column names after the repair?

Because what counts as one observation is a choice. For the slide, one observation is a category in a quarter, measured twice — hits rated excellent and hits rated good. Then Excellent and Good are two variables, and the table is tidy. If one observation is a category in a quarter with one rating, the fully long table Category | Quarter | Rating | #Hits is the tidy one. The deck allows the choice in its lessons learnt: "Shape may depend on convenience of queries" (p. 1140). The price is the one the widget shows: a third rating level means a new column, and every query that names the old ones must change.

A value never belongs in a header: when it sits there, new data means new columns, and every query that names them must be rewritten.

One kind of thing per table

The two remaining messy shapes are about observational units — the kind of thing one row describes. Page 1135 bills two customers. Its first table mixes two kinds of thing, invoices and invoice lines, so every fact about an invoice is repeated on each of its lines.

The slide's verdict is "Normalize to avoid duplication": one table per kind of observation, linked by the key Invoice. It is the normalization of lesson 9, met from the data side.

Why bother, if no number is lost? Because a fact stored twice can disagree with itself. An update that changes one copy — Jim Jones moves, the rate changes — leaves the other behind; lesson 9 calls it an update anomaly. Before reading on, check invoice 1 on the slide: two lines, 1 × 3.55 and 2 × 2.05, a Sales % of 8 and a Total($) of 8.03. Does the total agree with its lines?

Check yourself

No. The lines add up to 1 × 3.55 + 2 × 2.05 = 7.65, and 8 % on top gives 7.65 × 1.08 = 8.26. Invoice 2 follows the rule to the cent: (59.25 + 3 × 10.25) × 1.08 = 90.00 × 1.08 = 97.20. The printed 8.03 is 7.65 × 1.05, a 5 % rate.

Invoice "1", Bill To "Jim Jones", Sales % "8", Total($) "8.03" — printed on both lines of the flat table and in the normalized invoice table, with the lines "A123 Hammer 1 3.55" and "Q34 Screw Driver 2 2.05"

With its own lines and its own rate, invoice 1 totals (1 × 3.55 + 2 × 2.05) × 1.08 = 7.65 × 1.08 = 8.26. The printed 8.03 corresponds to a 5 % rate, not 8 %.

Invoice 2 on the same slide follows the 8 % rule to the cent (97.20), so invoice 1 is the odd one out. Same thing, different records: keys and duplicates

Combining sources raises a harder question than shape: which rows describe the same thing? Page 1137 puts it in four lines — "Keys are crucial in DB", "Many DBs --> Many keys", "How to align?", "Identification to certain degree of accuracy likely-identities" — with the example "same user match" over eight social-network logos.

Inside one database a key identifies a record exactly. Across databases there is no shared key: the same person is a numeric id on one network, a handle on another, an account name on a third. This is the key (matching) problem. With no key in common, records are compared on what they say — name, address, age, behaviour — and a pair similar enough becomes a likely identity. It is a decision that can be wrong, not a lookup. You met its rule-based form as the Matching step of cleansing, with its table of match patterns (p. 1094, lesson 35).

The duplicates problem of p. 1138 is the same question inside one data set: "Related to Key problem", again "Identification to certain degree of accuracy", now of "likely-duplicates", with the example "duplicate posts". A duplicate is a record that should share a key with another one and does not: the same post on four networks, the same customer registered twice with a typo. SELECT DISTINCT removes only identical copies; one letter of difference and it sees two different rows.

Likely duplicates: one threshold, two kinds of mistake

Two farmers' markets merge their lists of buyers — the People of the exam of 12 September 2025 (name, surname, age, address). A-xx come from market A, B-xx from market B, and their IDs do not match.   1. Compare on name and surname. At 1.00 three clusters appear. Chiara Rosi (A-06) is a typo for Chiara Rossi: her closest similarity is 0.917. Predict what happens as you lower the threshold towards 0.91 — and in which order.

    What you should see

    From 0.91 down to 0.60: no false merge — Claudia is 31, Claudio 34 — and one miss, Federico Bianchi, whom market A recorded at 45 and market B a year later at 46. Every rule errs somewhere; you choose where. That is "identification to certain degree of accuracy", and it is why a person confirms each cluster in a real tool. (A birth date instead of an age would have found Federico.)

The widget imitates a real menu: "Cluster and edit…", visible under "Edit cells" in the OpenRefine screenshot of p. 1147. OpenRefine groups values that collide on a fingerprint or lie within an edit distance, and a person accepts or rejects each proposed merge (background from the OpenRefine documentation, not the slides). The widget's own merging rule is the single linkage of Applied Statistics: two records closer than the threshold share a cluster, and closeness chains — A near B and B near C put all three together.

Why does the slide call matching and duplicates "crucial in data science"? Because they decide what you count. A missed match counts one customer twice; a false merge sends one person's purchases to another. In AN2DL terms: the same image left in both the training and the test split inflates the test accuracy.

Without a shared key, identity is a likelihood: every matching rule trades false merges against missed duplicates.

Why the reader has to wrangle: schema on read

Why does a course about databases spend a lecture on cleaning tables? Page 1142 gives the reason in three lines: "Traditional DBMSs enforced writing only data consistent with a pre-designed schema"; "Today data modeling at design time is a luxury"; "In schema on read, data is applied to a plan or schema as it is pulled out of a stored location, rather than as it goes in".

With schema on write the database is a gatekeeper: a row that does not fit the declared table is refused, so whatever is stored has already been shaped and typed — by whoever loaded it. The cost is design time: someone modelled the data before it arrived. That is the luxury. With many sources that change faster than anyone can agree on a schema, nobody gets that time.

Read the slide's last line as: the plan or schema is applied to the data when it is read.

With schema on read everything is stored as it comes, and a schema is applied only when the data is read. No designed schema stood in the way of the wide comedy table, the invoice with its repeated totals or the buyer registered twice: they were stored as they came. So every reader inherits the work of this lesson — tidy the shape, align the keys, merge the duplicates — and of lesson 36: fill the holes, question the outliers. Wrangling itself is not new: lesson 35 met it as "(good old) ETL", done once, before the load. Schema on read moves it to the moment of reading, and to every reader. Lesson 4 shows the same shift as two pipelines (pp. 169–170).

"Schema on read means there is no schema." The schema moves from the database to the reader; it does not disappear. The slide's own sentence names it: "a plan or schema", applied as the data is pulled out. Lesson 4 asks the same of NoSQL documents: "Schema-less or implicit schema?" (p. 168).

Explain the difference between the rows and columns of a table and its observations and variables. Then explain, with an example, the lessons learnt "Never values as columns!" and "Each variable belongs to (only!) one column!".

Show model answer

Rows and columns are the physical layout of a table; observations and variables are what its values mean. A variable is a property measured on every unit (school, year, student count); an observation is the set of values measured on one unit (one school in one year). A table is well shaped when each variable is one column and each observation one row. "Never values as columns!" forbids headers that are values of a variable: in School | 2012 | 2013 | 2014 the years are values of the variable year, so one row holds three observations, and a query cannot filter or group by year without listing columns by hand. Melting the headers gives School | Year | Student Count, one row per school and year. "Each variable belongs to (only!) one column!" forbids spreading one variable over several columns (Q1 | Q2 | Q3) and gluing two variables into one: Comedy-Q1 holds a category and a quarter, and must be split into a Category and a Quarter column.

A good answer has two parts: the physical-versus-semantic distinction, and one concrete repair for each lesson. Quote the two lessons exactly as printed (pp. 1140–1141); a closed question may reuse that wording.

Describe the "Key" (matching) problem and the "Duplicates" problem presented in the lecture. Why can they be solved only "to certain degree of accuracy"? Give an example of each.

Show model answer

Inside one database a key identifies each record exactly. When data comes from many databases, each has its own keys — a Facebook id, a Twitter handle — and nothing says which records denote the same real entity: aligning them is the key (matching) problem, for example matching the same user across social networks. The duplicates problem is the same question inside one data set: which records are the same observation recorded twice, for example the same post published on several networks, or a customer registered twice with a typo. Because no shared key exists, records are compared on their values (name, address, age) with a similarity measure, and a pair above a chosen threshold is declared a likely identity or a likely duplicate. The decision can be wrong in two ways: a low threshold merges different entities (false matches), a high one misses true duplicates. Exact-duplicate removal such as DISTINCT finds only identical copies.

A good answer separates aligning records of different sources (key problem) from finding repeated records in one data set (duplicates problem), says why no exact solution exists — no shared key, comparison by similarity against a threshold — and names the two kinds of error.

In the tidy-it widget, the hits table is at step 1, as printed, and a new year, 2016, arrives. Why does the wide query not break?

Among the data source selection criteria, "Currency" asks…

  • how much the data costs That is Cost, a separate criterion.
  • in which money unit the prices are stored That is a format question for cleansing, not a reason to choose a source.
  • whether the data is up to date Currency is up-to-dateness: last year's prices may be accurate and still useless.
  • whether you may legally use the data That is Legal issues, with Compliance.

Invoice 1 of p. 1135 stores its total on both of its lines. Which repair removes the duplicated copies of that total?

  • Normalize: an invoice table that holds the total once, and a line table linked to it by Invoice One table per kind of observation: each invoice fact is stored once.
  • Melt the Total($) column Total($) is a variable, not a value sitting in a header; there is nothing to melt.
  • Run SELECT DISTINCT on the table The two lines differ in SKU#, item, quantity and price: no row is an exact copy, so DISTINCT removes nothing.
  • Pivot the items into columns That would put values, the item names, into headers.

Even normalized, a stored total can disagree with the lines it is computed from — the printed 8.03 does. Computing it from the lines removes that last copy.

In the duplicates widget, comparing on name and surname, Claudia Trenti joins Claudio's cluster at 0.92, but Chiara Rosi joins Chiara Rossi only at 0.91. What does this show?

  • The similarity of the widget is miscomputed It is right: one edit in 14 characters (0.929) is more similar than one edit in 12 (0.917).
  • An edit distance cannot tell a typo from a different person: both pairs differ by one letter The string alone carries no meaning; another field, such as age, has to separate them.
  • Lowering the threshold always improves the result Lowering it catches more typos and makes more false merges.
  • Claudia is really a duplicate of Claudio Different people, different ages: the merge is false.

Why does schema on read make data wrangling the reader's job?

  • Because the database cleans the data while writing it No database cleans on its own. Under schema on write it refuses a row that does not fit, so the loader shapes the data before the write.
  • Because the schema is deleted after the write The schema is not deleted: it is applied later, when the data is read.
  • Because nothing checks the data when it is stored, so whoever reads it must shape and clean it "Data modeling at design time is a luxury": the schema, and the wrangling with it, move from the load to the reader.

You join the buyers of a market to a public registry of farms by approximate name matching. In the checklist of p. 1148 this step belongs to…

  • Data Exploration Exploration looks at data — visual inspection, graphing — without changing it.
  • Data Shaping Shaping tidies the form of one table.
  • Data Cleansing Cleansing repairs values within a source: missing values, formats, units, outliers, typos.
  • Data Augmenting "Aggregate data sources" and "Fuzzy/Exact match": you match records in order to add a source.

Key take-aways

  • Before any table, the slides sort values into categorical (Color), qualitative and subjective (Nice, Good, For birthday) and quantitative, discrete (6 balloons) or continuous (15 PSI, 139 m) — the type decides which cleaning makes sense; twelve selection criteria decide whether a source is worth cleaning at all, Currency meaning up to date.
  • Rows and columns are physical, observations and variables semantic: a table is well shaped when each variable is one column and each observation one row — "Never values as columns!".
  • The four messy shapes have four repairs: values in headers are melted into a column, variables in both rows and columns are melted and pivoted, several observational units in one table are normalized, and one unit spread over many tables is combined with the period as a column.
  • Without a shared key, records can be matched only to a degree of accuracy — likely identities across sources, likely duplicates within one — and every rule trades false merges against missed duplicates.
  • Schema on read stores data as it comes ("data modeling at design time is a luxury") and applies the schema as the data is pulled out, so the wrangling that ETL once did before the load falls on every reader.
  • The wrangling tools — R and RStudio, Python and pandas, OpenRefine, Trifacta and the other vendors — script or record every transformation as a replayable step (Trifacta also profiles every column), along the checklist ingestion, exploration, shaping, cleansing, augmenting.

    The paper behind the rules of pp. 1132–1141. Wickham counts five messy patterns — this lesson's four, with the glued header (Comedy-Q1) as a pattern of its own — and shows why tidy data is close to third normal form.

The open-source tools of this lesson — pandas, R, OpenRefine — work on a table that fits in one machine's memory.
